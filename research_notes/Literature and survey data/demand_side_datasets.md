# Demand-side datasets and market-sizing evidence for OMP (research round 2, 2026-10-15)

Scope: existing surveys, government statistics, trade-association reports and academic studies that could size or describe demand for a free, open-source live-music platform whose v1 serves US school and community ensembles, with an artist-to-venue settlement tool second. US focus; UK/EU/Australia marked as comparators. Figures marked "vendor claim" are a company's own marketing. Where a PDF was read in full (HEADS 2019-20, EdTA 2012, Give a Note 2017, SPPA 2022 highlights, NCS-IV, MVT 2025, Live DMA 2019-2022), the numbers below were taken from the extracted text, not from search snippets.

## 1. Music education and ensemble activity sizing (US)

### Takeaway
No US dataset counts K-12 or college concerts, recitals or performances per year. The best you can do is count programs, ensembles and enrolled students, and multiply by a concerts-per-program rate that you would have to measure yourself (the educator survey). The strongest program counts come from Give a Note 2017 (school-level ensemble offering rates, nationally weighted), the Arts Education Data Project (state enrollment), and NASM HEADS (college music units, enrollment, and a ticket-income line). HEADS turns out to be the only source found that puts a dollar figure on music-unit ticket revenue.

### Cited Findings

**NCES Fast Response Survey System (FRSS), "Arts Education in Public Elementary and Secondary Schools: 1999–2000 and 2009–10"** (publisher: NCES / US Dept. of Education; latest year: 2009–10; access: free public-use files)
- Seven FRSS surveys ran in 2009–10, including separate files for elementary and secondary music specialists. They measure whether music is offered, staffing, and instruction time, not performances. — [GIA summary of NCES report](https://www.giarts.org/node/2919); [NCES public-use data file FRSS 100](https://nces.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/data/data-file/public-use-data-files-and-documentation-frss-100-elementary-school-arts-education-survey-fall-2009)
- No newer FRSS arts survey was found. The most recent NCES arts collection is arts items in the **School Pulse Panel (November 2024)**, with more than 4,000 public K-12 schools responding. The NEA published a brief on it, and the data can be downloaded from NCES. The brief's landing page reports no performance or ticketing items. — [NEA brief on School Pulse Panel Nov 2024](https://www.arts.gov/impact/research/publications/arts-education-us-public-schools-insights-november-2024-school-pulse-panel-survey)
- The Arts Education Partnership's 2024 inventory says most arts-ed data collection covers in-school settings, with less data on out-of-school learning. — [AEP, Arts Education Data and Reporting Initiatives 2024](https://www.aep-arts.org/wp-content/uploads/Arts-Education-Data-and-Reporting-Initiatives-2024.pdf)

**Arts Education Data Project (AEDP)** (publisher: State Education Agency Directors of Arts Education (SEADAE) and Quadrant Research; latest: state dashboards updated per state, national report on 2018–19 data; access: free state dashboards, custom reports on request)
- AEDP says it works in 31 states. It reports course offerings, enrollment, teacher counts and access by school, and the metrics vary by state (Ohio: offerings, enrollment, teachers; New Jersey: participation and students without access). — [artseddata.org](https://artseddata.org/)
- The national report (2018–19 data, 17 states, 30,633 schools, 7,015 districts, 18 million students) found 92% of public-school students had access to music, while about 3.6 million did not. — [AEDP national study release](https://artseddata.org/?p=675)
- Arizona example: about 60% of students (about 643,000) took arts courses in 2020–21, with high schools lowest at 37%. Data are self-reported by districts. — [Arizona Arts Ed Report 2020–21](https://www.azed.gov/sites/default/files/media/AZ%20Arts%20Ed%20Report%202020-21.pdf)

**Give a Note Foundation, "The Status of Music Education in United States Public Schools – 2017"** (publishers: Give a Note Foundation and CMA Foundation; fielded 2017; access: free PDF via UMD DRUM)
- Sample: 468 music teachers in 392 public schools, weighted to represent about 103,000 public schools and about 117,000 public-school music teachers. — [Give a Note 2017, UMD DRUM](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/20212)
- Ensemble offering rates: at elementary schools, concert band 43% and choir 39%. Band is offered at 91% of middle schools and 93% of high schools, and orchestra at 41% of middle schools. Rates are among schools with music programs. — [Give a Note 2017](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/20212)
- Fundraising, the nearest item to performance cash flow: student fundraising supplements budgets at 79% of high schools and parent fundraising at 63%. 38% of high schools and 34% of middle schools get the majority of music funding from fundraised sources. 11% of high schools raise more than $10,000 a year, and the plurality (41%) raise $1,001–$5,000. — [Give a Note 2017](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/20212)
- The report has **no concert count and no ticket-sales or admission item** (checked against the full text).

**Elpus & Abril, "Who Enrolls in High School Music? A National Profile of U.S. Students, 2009–2013"** (Journal of Research in Music Education, 2019; uses federal longitudinal transcript data; access: article paywalled, underlying NCES data restricted-use license)
- 24% of the class of 2013 took at least one year of band, choir or orchestra in high school: choir 13%, band 11%, orchestra 2%. — [SAGE, JRME 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022429419862837); [Teaching Music summary, Apr 2020](https://digitaleditions.walsworth.com/article/Who+Enrolls+In+High+School+Music%3F+A+New+National+Profile/3626697/653680/article.html)
- The earlier Elpus & Abril (2011) study found 21% of the class of 2004 in school ensembles. — [K-State copy of 2011 JRME article](https://www.k-state.edu/musiceducation/phillippayne/Phillip_Payne/2013_CBC_Clinic_files/HS%20Music%20Demographics.pdf)
- Caveat: the search snippets did not show the 2019 article's author line. The attribution to Elpus & Abril comes from the brief and the Teaching Music summary.

**NASM Higher Education Arts Data Services (HEADS), Music Data Summaries** (publisher: National Association of Schools of Music, with NASAD/NAST/NASD; summaries cover 1982–83 through 2020–21, newer years on dashboards; access: summary PDFs free; dashboard access terms not confirmed)
- The 2019–20 summary covers 606 institutions: 101,545 music students enrolled in fall 2019 (all levels) and 20,709 degrees, certificates or diplomas awarded July 2018–June 2019. — [HEADS Music Data Summaries 2019–2020 (PDF)](https://nasm.arts-accredit.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2023/07/M-2019-2020-HEADS-Data-Summaries.pdf)
- **Performance-related income (Chart 26, "Specific income sources for the music unit in 2018–2019", line "Tickets/Commission/Rentals")**, all institutions: 394 units reporting, 25th percentile $6,059, median $15,700, 75th percentile $42,825, 95th percentile $256,928, mean $67,000. Private institutions (148 reporting): median $12,667. Public (246): median $19,613. — [HEADS 2019–2020](https://nasm.arts-accredit.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2023/07/M-2019-2020-HEADS-Data-Summaries.pdf)
- The HEADS questionnaire collects enrollment, faculty, budgets, and "Ticket Sales, Commissions, Rentals, Etc. (income earned by the music unit related to performance)" (Section VI.E). It has **no field for the number of concerts, recitals or ensembles**. — [HEADS 2020–21 survey, Arkansas Tech printable copy](https://www.atu.edu/humanities/music/nasm/2023/docs/2020-2021%20NASM%20Survey%20Arkansas%20Tech.html); [NASM HEADS overview](https://nasm.arts-accredit.org/services/heads/)

**League of American Orchestras, "Orchestras at a Glance"** (latest edition Oct 2024, 2022–23 data; free PDF)
- About 2,200 professional, community and youth orchestras in the US, with 2,218 mapped through a League and SMU DataArts partnership. — [Orchestras at a Glance, Oct 2024](https://americanorchestras.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Orchestras-at-a-Glance-Oct-2024.pdf)
- The 2020 edition estimated about 1,600 orchestras from a 138-orchestra survey of 2016–17. The two counts are not comparable: the newer one uses an IRS-based universe. — [Orchestras at a Glance 2020](https://americanorchestras.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Orchestras-at-a-Glance-2020.pdf)

**Association of Concert Bands (ACB)**
- An NPR story citing ACB said there are more than 1,000 community bands in the US. This is a lower bound and a secondary citation. — [NPR via Hawaii Public Radio, 2022](https://www.hawaiipublicradio.org/2022-09-07/community-bands-are-back-after-being-battered-by-the-pandemic)
- Wikipedia's figure of about 2,500 has no source and should not be used. — [Wikipedia, Community band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_band)

**Chorus America, "Chorus Impact Study: Singing for a Lifetime"** (2019, online survey Nov 2018 by Grunwald Associates; free summary)
- More than 54 million Americans (adults and children) sing in choruses. Adult participation rose from 14% (2008) to 17%. — [Chorus America](https://chorusamerica.org/resource/chorus-impact-study-singing-lifetime); [press release PDF](https://chorusamerica.org/sites/default/files/CIS_SingingforaLifetime_pressrelease_final.pdf)
- No count of choruses was found in the 2019 materials.

**National Congregations Study, Wave IV (2018–19)** (Duke University, Mark Chaves et al.; report 2021/22; free PDF)
- The share of congregations with choir singing at the main worship service was 53.9% in 1998, 44.1%, 45.3%, and 41.9% in 2018–19. Excluding summer interviews, the 2018–19 figure is 46.2%. The Wave IV sample is 1,262 congregations. The report refers to "more than 300,000 congregations in the United States." — [NCS-IV report](https://sites.duke.edu/ncsweb/files/2022/02/NCSIV_Report_Web_FINAL2.pdf)
- The report has no item on concerts or concert series hosted by congregations (searched the full text).

**NEA SPPA 2022, venue question** (see Q3 for details)
- 4.4% of US adults attended an arts event at an elementary, middle or high school, 5.8% at a college or university, and 4.3% at a place of worship. However, the performing-arts attendance questions told respondents to **omit "performances by elementary, middle, or high school student groups."** — [NEA, 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible sizing chain is: about 103,000 public schools (Give a Note frame) × ensemble offering rates (91–93% band at secondary schools with music) × concerts per ensemble per year (**unknown, so it has to come from OMP's own educator survey**). Private schools, colleges (about 600 HEADS units), about 2,200 orchestras, more than 1,000 community bands, and choruses and congregations would be added on top. The only parameter with no public source is concerts per program per year.
- The HEADS ticket line gives a revenue ceiling for colleges. 394 units reporting a median $15,700 a year in "tickets/commissions/rentals" (rentals included) suggests per-unit ticket volume is modest. Even if a fee were charged, small-percentage fees on these amounts are trivial, which supports the free-tool positioning more than a revenue model.
- SPPA systematically under-counts the K-12 concert audience, because its instructions exclude student-group performances. Any use of SPPA for school-concert demand must say so.

### Gaps
- **Not found: any US count of K-12 or college concerts or recitals per year.** NCES, AEDP, Give a Note and HEADS all lack a performance-count field.
- Not found: an AEDP list of which states report ensemble-level (band/choir/orchestra) enrollment, rather than "music" overall. The artseddata.org home page lists 31 participating states but not their metrics.
- Not found: an ACB primary count of community bands, a current Chorus America count of choruses, or College Music Society data on performances. The CMS site was not reached this round.
- Whether the November 2024 School Pulse Panel arts items break out music, or ask about performances, was not confirmed. The NEA's PDF brief (`/sites/default/files/SchoolPulseSurvey_final.pdf`) was not read.

## 2. School and arts ticketing practices

### Takeaway
There is **no survey of how school music programs sell tickets or handle cash**. The closest analog is the EdTA/Utah State 2012 survey of high school theatre. It shows box office income is nearly universal (89% of theatre teachers receive it) and is the only funding source most programs get regularly or substantially. It does not ask how tickets are sold (online vs. door, cash vs. card). State auditor reports document recurring weaknesses in controls over school gate and ticket cash, but only as individual cases, mostly athletics, with no aggregate frequency.

### Cited Findings

**EdTA / Utah State University, "Survey of Theatre Education in United States High Schools 2011–12"** (Omasta et al.; published in Teaching Theatre 2012; access: free PDF; survey instruments at schooltheatre.org)
- Method: a modified census of every US public high school with at least 200 students, by mail and online, Nov 2011–Jun 2012. Administrator and teacher surveys came from more than 1,200 schools (about 835 administrators, 864 teachers at schools producing plays). The authors caution that not all schools opted in, so the sample is self-selected. — [EdTA 2012 survey (Americans for the Arts copy)](https://ww2.americansforthearts.org/sites/default/files/assets/files/Survey%20of%20theatre%20education%20in%20United%20States%20high%20schools%202012_web.pdf); [EdTA landscape survey page](https://community.schooltheatre.org/old/advocacy/landscapesurvey)
- "The only funding source from which most schools received regular or substantial support was ticket sales": 89% of teachers received box office revenue, and only 4% reported none. — [EdTA 2012](https://ww2.americansforthearts.org/sites/default/files/assets/files/Survey%20of%20theatre%20education%20in%20United%20States%20high%20schools%202012_web.pdf)
- 75% held fundraising events at least occasionally, but only 38% got regular or substantial funds from them. About a third got regular or substantial district support. Occasional sources: individual donors 75%, program advertising 56%, parent boosters 46%, space rental 16%. 19% charge students a participation fee. — [EdTA 2012](https://ww2.americansforthearts.org/sites/default/files/assets/files/Survey%20of%20theatre%20education%20in%20United%20States%20high%20schools%202012_web.pdf)
- Median productions per year: 5 (4 when one-acts and scenes are weighted 0.5). Average spend: $7,510 per musical and $2,701 per full-length non-musical. Only 6% said admission profits go to non-theatre accounts. 38% must cover end-of-year deficits. — [EdTA 2012](https://ww2.americansforthearts.org/sites/default/files/assets/files/Survey%20of%20theatre%20education%20in%20United%20States%20high%20schools%202012_web.pdf)
- The published survey does not report ticketing channel, payment method, ticket price, or attendance per show (checked against the full text).
- Vendor comparator (Ticketpeak, not EdTA): school theatre average revenue per ticket was $11.28 and the median $10.67, net of comps. — [Ticketpeak analysis](https://ticketpeak.com/the-most-successful-plays-musicals-produced-in-schools/)

**State auditor and comptroller reports on school activity funds** (various states; free)
- New York OSC (extraclassroom activity funds): of 442 cash receipts totaling $299,000, 158 ($113,800) were unsupported and 193 ($164,400) were late or could not be checked for timeliness. — [NY OSC 2016 global audit](https://osc.state.ny.us/localgov/audits/swr/2016/extraclassroom/global.htm)
- Ohio (Switzerland of Ohio LSD): a $5,464 finding for recovery where ticket money from games had no deposit record. The auditor flagged weak controls at athletic cash collection points "across the board." — [Ohio Auditor press release](https://ohioauditor.gov/news/pressreleases/Details/5406)
- Alabama: one person collected and counted gate receipts. Auditors recommended two-person counts, pre-numbered tickets and reconciliation. — [Alabama LEA audit findings](https://alabamaachieves.org/lea-fiscal-accountability/audit-findings)
- Louisiana (Mt. Hermon School): ticket reconciliation forms were not used and football receipts had no reconciliation. Indiana: ticket counts retained did not match deposits for two events. Mississippi (Harrison County SD): activity fund receipts flagged as a significant deficiency. — [Louisiana LLA report](https://lla.la.gov/PublicReports.nsf/0/27AFC2CA9E8A935B862577900061EB07/$FILE/000192A8.pdf); [Indiana SBOA B50542](https://www.in.gov/sboa/WebReports/B50542.pdf); [Mississippi OSA](https://www.osa.ms.gov/sites/default/files/osa/files/reports/23com2023%20Harrison%20County%20School%20District.pdf)

**School ticketing vendors** (vendor claims only)
- GoFan (PlayOn): more than 10,000 high schools served and more than 30 million tickets sold. It covers athletics and "performing arts," with no breakdown for performing arts. — [PlayOn about](https://playonsports.com/company/about); [GoFan](https://gofan.playonsports.com/)
- HomeTown Ticketing: "thousands of educational organizations," with no published figures. — [UIS release](https://www.uis.edu/es/node/9954)

**Other**
- Give a Note 2017 (Q1) is the only nationally representative source on music-program cash flow. It covers fundraising, not ticketing.
- HEADS (Q1) gives college music-unit ticket, commission and rental income.

### Inferences
- The EdTA finding transfers to music only partly. School theatre is a ticketed product with royalties to pay, while many school concerts are free or donation-based. Whether music concerts charge admission at the theatre rate is unknown. That is exactly what the OMP educator survey should measure.
- Auditor findings support the idea that handling cash at the door is a known control problem for schools. They are drawn mostly from athletics gates and sampled case by case, so they cannot support a prevalence claim.

### Gaps
- **Not found: any survey of music educators' ticketing practices** (paid vs. free concerts, online vs. door, cash vs. card, who handles money).
- Not found: an ASBO International guidance document or survey on activity-fund ticketing. asbointl.org was not reached this round.
- Not found: any NFHS dataset on school performing-arts ticketing.
- Not found: published statistics from Ludus, On the Stage or ShowTix4U on school performing-arts volume.
- Not found: an EdTA survey newer than 2012 with box-office detail. The 2021–22 EdTA play survey statement notes box office took a "lingering hit," with no figures. A 2026 AACT (community theatre) education survey PDF exists but was not read: [AACT education survey 2026](https://aact.org/sites/default/files/2026-05/education-survey-2026.pdf).

## 3. Live-music audience and attendance data

### Takeaway
The NEA's SPPA 2022 is the authoritative US attendance dataset: n = 40,718, free microdata via ICPSR/NADAC. It reports attendance by genre and venue type, but excludes K-12 student-group performances and has no paid-vs-free item in the highlights. AEP6 gives per-attendee spending at nonprofit arts events. Industry sources (Eventbrite, NIVA/Bandsintown) are vendor or association data with thin methodology.

### Cited Findings

**NEA Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (SPPA) 2022** (NEA with Census Bureau, CPS supplement July 2022; highlights published 2023; microdata ICPSR 38936 v2 dated Feb 2026, free via NADAC)
- Sample: 40,718 adults (23.2% by proxy), person response rate 59.1%. — [NEA 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)
- 2022 attendance: jazz 6.3%, Latin/Spanish/salsa 3.9%, classical 4.6%, musical stage play 10.3%. "Other" live music, dance or theater (rock, pop, country, hip-hop, comedy and so on) 21%, up from 15% in 2017. — [NEA 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)
- Venue types (share of adults attending arts events there): open-air facility 17.1%, restaurant/bar/nightclub/coffee shop 10.3%, college 5.8%, community center or library 4.9%, K-12 school 4.4%, place of worship 4.3%. — [NEA 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)
- The performing-arts attendance items told respondents to omit "performances by elementary, middle, or high school student groups." — [NEA 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)
- More than 17% first heard about an attended event through social media, and 15% through friends, neighbors or co-workers. — [NEA 2022 SPPA highlights](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/2022-SPPA-final.pdf)
- Data access: ICPSR study 38936, free download from NADAC. — [ICPSR 38936](https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/NADAC/studies/38936)

**Americans for the Arts, Arts & Economic Prosperity 6 (AEP6)** (FY2022 data, attendee surveys Feb–Jul 2023; national and local summaries free)
- The typical attendee spends $38.46 per person per event, per the Arts Consulting Group summary (the snippet was truncated). — [ACG Arts Insights on AEP6](https://artsconsulting.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Arts-Insights-Volume-XXXIII-Issue-3-The-Economic-Impact-of-Americas-Nonprofit-Arts-and-Culture-Industry-1.pdf)
- Local example (Delaware): $35.32 for local attendees, $67.47 for nonlocal, $43.45 overall. Food and drink is the largest category. — [Delaware AEP6 summary](https://arts.delaware.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/84/2023/10/DE_StateOfDelaware_AEP6_OnePageSummaryOfFindings-1.pdf)

**Eventbrite Music Fans Report 2025** (vendor)
- 35 million ticket transactions for music events in 2024, drawn from a platform averaging 89 million monthly users. — [Eventbrite Music Fans Report 2025](https://assets.eventbrite.com/hubfs/Eventbrite-Music-Fans-Report-2025.pdf)

**NIVA and Bandsintown "Live Pulse"** (monthly, launched June 2026)
- The inaugural edition had 212+ independent venues, promoters and festivals. Methodology not found. — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/niva-bandsintown-survey-tracks-state-of-indie-live-events/)

### Inferences
- SPPA cannot size K-12 concert audiences directly because of the student-group exclusion. Its venue-type item (4.4% of adults attended something at a K-12 school) is the nearest proxy, but it measures non-student-group events at schools. ICPSR microdata would show whether any item captures student performances. The highlights suggest not.
- For community ensembles, places of worship (4.3%) and community centers or libraries (4.9%) are a combined national audience reach of a similar order to college venues.

### Gaps
- Not found: any SPPA 2022 item on whether admission was free or paid. The highlights report has none, and the codebook was not inspected.
- Not found: a Luminate live-attendance dataset. Luminate's year-end reports are consumption-focused. Pollstar box-office data cover mainly reporting venues and tours, which is a known limitation, and were not examined this round.
- Not found: an AEP6 breakdown by free vs. ticketed events, or by music discipline. Whether AEP6 spending excludes admission was not verified.
- Not found: the NEA Arts Basic Survey (ABS) figures this round.

## 4. Small-venue economics and fee dependence

### Takeaway
No US source publishes small-venue income splits or ticket-fee revenue. NIVA's State of Live (2025) released only headline economic-impact figures, and no methodology or fee data were found. The best quantified comparators are European and UK: Live DMA (2,280 venues, 2019 data with 2022 updates) and the Music Venue Trust 2025 report (801 UK grassroots venues). Both show that ticket sales are about a third of venue income and that small venues lose money on live music.

### Cited Findings

**NIVA "State of Live"** (survey open Jan 7–Feb 18, 2025; for-profit and nonprofit; national plus 50 state reports released June 2025)
- Launch details and eligibility (any size, tax status or location). — [NIVA launch statement](https://nivassoc.org/statements/2025/the-national-independent-venue-association-launches-first-ever-economic-research-survey-of-independent-live-sector)
- Headline: independent venues generated $153.1B in total economic output in 2024, and 64% of stages are struggling with unprofitability. — [Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/?p=1201303)
- The NIVA news page for the findings returned 404, so methodology, n, and any ticket-fee revenue share were **not found**.

**Live DMA, "Live music venues and clubs face rising costs amid recovery" (data 2019–2022, published Dec 2024)** (EU comparator; free PDF)
- 2019 data from 522 venues across 10 countries, extrapolated to 2,280 venues: 281,000 music events, 463,000 artist performances, 70 million audience visits. €1.7B income, of which €563M was tickets, €551M food and beverage, and €351M subsidies. — [Live DMA 2019–2022 report](https://cnm.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/LIVE-DMA_LIVE-MUSIC-VENUES-AND-CLUBS-FACE-RISING-COSTS-AMID-RECOVERY_DATA2019-2022_web.pdf)
- Income mix: 33% tickets, 33% food and beverage, 21% subsidy overall. By venue type:

  | Venue type | Tickets | Food and beverage | Subsidy |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Private commercial | 42% | 43% | 4% |
  | Private non-profit | 29% | 15% | 31% |
  | Public | 19% | 15% | 51% |

  — [Live DMA 2019–2022](https://cnm.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/LIVE-DMA_LIVE-MUSIC-VENUES-AND-CLUBS-FACE-RISING-COSTS-AMID-RECOVERY_DATA2019-2022_web.pdf)
- In 2019, 80% of audiences paid admission and 20% entered free (15% at commercial venues, 21% at public, 24% at private non-profit). 88% of ticket income is spent on programme costs, and the smaller the venue, the less of its programme costs ticket sales cover. Programme costs include "partage payments" (door splits) and booking fees. The "ticket sales" definition does not break out booking or service fees. — [Live DMA 2019–2022](https://cnm.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/LIVE-DMA_LIVE-MUSIC-VENUES-AND-CLUBS-FACE-RISING-COSTS-AMID-RECOVERY_DATA2019-2022_web.pdf)
- Earlier (2017 data, published Jan 2020): small venues earned 23% from tickets and 29% from catering, and tickets covered only 82% of programme costs at small venues, versus 116% at large ones. — [The Ticketing Business](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2020/01/21/ticket-sales-account-third-income-european-live-music-venues/)
- Data for 2023 and 2024 are not yet available for most countries (as of the Dec 2024 report). — [Live DMA 2019–2022](https://cnm.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/LIVE-DMA_LIVE-MUSIC-VENUES-AND-CLUBS-FACE-RISING-COSTS-AMID-RECOVERY_DATA2019-2022_web.pdf)

**Music Venue Trust Annual Report 2025** (UK comparator; survey of Music Venues Alliance members, n described only as "statistically significant"; published Jan 2026; free)
- 801 grassroots venues, average capacity 316. 174,552 events, of which 95,696 were ticketed live music and 27,381 free-entry live music. Ticketed live music was 54.8% of all events and free live music 15.7%. The free-entry share fell from 35.2% (2024) to 24.2%. — [MVT Annual Report 2025 (PDF)](https://vnpf.nl/content/uploads/2026/01/MVT_2025-Annual-Report_Digital-Spreads.pdf); [MVT summary](https://www.musicvenuetrust.com/2026/01/music-venue-trust-annual-report-2025/)
- 32.1% of income came from ticket sales. The average ticket price was £11.56 (up from £11.48). Ticketed shows averaged 122 attendees at 38.7% capacity. Each event averaged 8.6 artists. — [MVT 2025](https://vnpf.nl/content/uploads/2026/01/MVT_2025-Annual-Report_Digital-Spreads.pdf)
- 53.8% of venues reported no profit, and the average margin was 2.5%. Live music income was £179.2M against £255.9M live music expenditure, a sector "subsidy" of £76.6M, or an average loss on live music of £95,685 per venue. — [MVT 2025](https://vnpf.nl/content/uploads/2026/01/MVT_2025-Annual-Report_Digital-Spreads.pdf)

**US city music censuses**
- Greater Austin Music Census 2022 (Sound Music Cities; about 2,200–2,300 respondents, with outlets disagreeing on the count): 38% struggled to afford housing. About 18% of venue operators reported falling income over three years. Audiences are increasingly reluctant to pay cover for local music. Almost 70% of musicians made under $10,000 a year from music (secondary write-up). Outlets disagree on whether detailed earnings were asked. — [KUT](https://www.kut.org/post/one-fifth-austins-musicians-live-below-federal-poverty-level-census-says); [Austin Monitor](https://austinmonitor.com/?p=134520); [Texas Monthly](https://www.texasmonthly.com/the-daily-post/eight-things-we-learned-about-austins-music-industry-from-the-austin-music-census/)
- Sacramento Music Census 2022 data deck exists (not read this round). — [Sacramento data deck appendix](https://www.cityofsacramento.gov/content/dam/portal/ccs/office-of-arts-and-culture/music-census/2022-August-Sacramento-Data-Deck-Appendix.pdf)

### Inferences
- If US small venues resemble Live DMA's private commercial venues or MVT's grassroots venues, ticket income covers roughly a third of revenue and live music runs at a loss subsidized by the bar. A settlement tool that does not take a cut of ticket revenue fits venues whose margins are around 2.5%.
- MVT's sharp fall in free-entry events (35% to 24%) suggests venues are moving toward ticketing even at the grassroots level. This is UK data only.

### Gaps
- **Not found: any US figure for the share of small shows that are door-only or cash-only**, or for the share of venue revenue from ticket or service fees. NIVA's methodology and fee data were not retrievable.
- Not found: Sound Diplomacy city studies with venue income splits. sounddiplomacy.com was not reached this round. Seattle, Portland, Philadelphia and Chicago census data were not checked.
- The Austin census primary report was not read, and secondary figures conflict.

## 5. Independent artist pay and settlement

### Takeaway
US income surveys exist (MIRA 2018, FMC 2011–12) and show that live performance is the most common income source. **No US dataset measures how often artists are paid late, short, or not at all by venues or promoters.** The only frequency-type figures are Australian (MEAA). Newer surveys (Ditto, Pirate) are global or UK-weighted vendor surveys on touring profitability, not settlement.

### Cited Findings

**MIRA / Princeton Survey Research Center / MusiCares 2018 US Musician Survey** (1,227 US musicians, fielded Apr 12–Jun 2, 2018; executive summary free)
- The median musician earned about $35,000 in 2017, of which about $21,300 came from music. Live performance was the most commonly reported music income source (81%), followed by lessons (47%) and religious-service performance (38%). 61% said music income was insufficient for living expenses. The sample includes aspiring as well as professional musicians. — [Krueger presentation slides](https://www.hypebot.com/content/files/musicbusinessresearch-files-wordpress-com/2018/09/presentation-slides-krueger.pdf); [Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2018/06/27/music-industry-research-association-income-study/); [AEA 2020 paper listing](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2020/preliminary/paper/Arez6hTd)
- No newer MIRA US musician survey was found.

**Future of Music Coalition, Artist Revenue Streams** (survey Sep 6–Oct 28, 2011, plus financial case studies 2012)
- An online survey of US musicians and composers, 18 and over. Case studies found live performance is the essential revenue stream for performers and that touring expenses often exceed touring income. — [GIA notice](https://www.giarts.org/node/2451); [Hypebot on case studies](https://www.hypebot.com/how-do-musicians-really-make-their-living-case-studies)

**Pirate (rehearsal-studio chain) 2024 survey** (about 1,700 bands, solo artists and DJs; geography mixed or unclear)
- 72% made zero profit touring, 48% broke even, 24% ran at a loss. 54% said gig fees stayed the same despite higher ticket prices, and 29% said they fell. — [DJ Mag](https://djmag.com/news/84-of-independent-artists-uk-cant-afford-tour-survey-finds); [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/despite-higher-ticket-prices-musicians-earnings-remain-stagnant.md)

**Ditto Music surveys** (vendor; global)
- Jan 2025: 82.1% of unsigned musicians globally cannot afford to tour (84% in the UK). — [Ditto press](https://press.dittomusic.com/82-of-independent-artists-cant-afford-to-tour-in-2025)
- 2024 (2,000+ musicians): 27.5% said most earnings came from live shows. — [Ditto press](https://press.dittomusic.com/independent-artists-make-more-from-streaming-than-merch-touring-and-publishing)
- Independent Music Report, Sep 2026 (5,000+ artists, 112 countries): 74% earned under $1,000 from music, and 64% hold a non-music job. — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/74-of-musicians-earned-under-1k-82-cant-afford-to-tour/)

**MEAA / Musicians Australia** (Australian comparator)
- 2025 survey (300+): 68% were paid for every gig, up from 53% in 2020. 2024 survey (550+): 42% played unpaid gigs. — [MEAA media release](https://www.meaa.org/?p=54219); [Beat on MEAA survey](https://beat.com.au/?p=461901)
- Canberra: dozens of musicians reported late or unpaid venue invoices, attributed to venue cash flow (anecdotal). — [Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/8634482/canberra-musicians-unpaid-struggles)
- AMIN national live-remuneration survey (covering pay rates and agreement types) was launched, but results were not found. — [The Music Network](https://themusicnetwork.com/news/live-remuneration-survey)

**UMAW** (US)
- UMAW cites venue merch cuts of 15–35%, with reports up to 40%. Its #MyMerch campaign had 160 venues pledge no merch fees. No UMAW survey on guarantees or settlement was found. — [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/arts/13935406/the-fillmore-will-stop-taking-a-cut-of-artists-merch-sales)

### Inferences
- MIRA shows live performance is near-universal as an income source, so a settlement tool targets the most widely shared revenue stream. MIRA's 38% earning from religious services also overlaps OMP's church and community-series segment.
- The Australian MEAA figure (about a third of musicians not paid for every gig in 2025) is the only quantified non-payment signal. It must not be presented as a US figure.

### Gaps
- **Not found: any US frequency data on late, short or missing payment from venues or promoters.** This holds across MIRA, FMC, UMAW, Austin census coverage, and vendor surveys.
- Not checked this round: MusiCares wellness surveys, Sound Diplomacy musician surveys, and academic work on settlement disputes (Google Scholar not queried).
- Not found: the FMC survey's final sample size and live-income share (the full report was not reached).

## 6. Artist access to fan data

### Takeaway
There is advocacy and now a legal hook (the Live Nation/DOJ settlement clause, plus NITO's data-share push), but **no survey quantifying what share of artists receive ticket-buyer data**. MIDiA's relevant reports are paywalled and their public summaries do not address data ownership.

### Cited Findings
- NITO's 2023 nine-point reform plan includes the ability for artists and their representatives to access their own ticket-buyer data, alongside fee caps. — [Pollstar 2023](https://news.pollstar.com/2023/04/20/nito-nine-point-ticketing-reform-plan-includes-caps-on-ticketing-fees-secondary-market-regulation/)
- NITO (March 2026) publicly called on ticketers to share buyer data with artists. It created a data-share agreement and distributed it to members. — [Pollstar guest post, 2026-03-31](https://news.pollstar.com/2026/03/31/nito-call-on-ticketers-to-share-buyer-data-with-artists-guest-post); [New Industry Focus](https://newindustryfocus.com/articles/nito-advocates-for-the-sharing-of-ticket-buyer-data-with-artists)
- The Live Nation/DOJ settlement includes a clause requiring buyer data to be shared with artists on request, under privacy protections and a DOJ-approved NDA. NIVA criticized the settlement for lacking protections for independent venues. — [Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/live-nation-settlement-transparency-clause-is-a-win-for-artists)
- MIDiA's superfan reports (Q3 2023 US/UK; 2024, 2,009 US consumers) are paywalled, and their summaries do not cover data ownership. — [MIDiA report page](https://www.midiaresearch.com/reports/superstars-and-their-superfans-turning-point)

### Inferences
- Because data sharing is now a negotiated or settlement-imposed exception for the largest promoter, the default for small shows is plausibly that artists get no buyer data. That is inference only, with no survey support.

### Gaps
- **Not found: any survey measuring how often artists receive ticket-buyer data.** Bandsintown and Songkick statements and academic work on platform data control in music were not found this round.
