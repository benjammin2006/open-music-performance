## Takeaway

- **This paper is a map of where to look. It contains no data for OMP.** It is a grounded-theory synthesis of 145 English-language, peer-reviewed business/management articles from 2017–2022. The content is about the recorded-music industry (digitalization, major-label oligopoly, streaming), festival management (experience, segmentation, sustainability), musician identity (bohemian vs entrepreneur), and in-store background music.
- **It has nothing on ticketing operations, fees, cash, settlement, fan-data ownership, crypto/NFT, music education operations, school or community ensembles, or US small venues.** Its search (`music AND (business OR management)`) and its business-school framing filtered these topics out. Its own limitations section excludes books and grey literature (p. 1446), which is where most ticketing and venue-economics evidence would sit.
- **Where it helps OMP most:**
  - **H (musician identity and entrepreneurship).** This is one of its three core themes.
  - **I (festival management)**, but only for experience, segmentation and sustainability. It says nothing on volunteers, cash or ticketing ops.
  - **J (platform/business models)**, through a few cited works.
- **Highest-value citation to chase:** Azzellini, Greer & Umney (2021), "Why isn't there an Uber for live music? The digitalisation of intermediaries and the limits of the platform economy," *New Technology, Work and Employment* 37(1):1–23. Pizzolitto cites it only once (p. 1440: "the world of live music has resisted such changes"). Going by the title, it is directly about why live-music intermediation resists platformization. That bears on B, D, E, F and J. **[Inference from title]**
- **The 145-article coding sheet is available on request.** The paper says the 19-column spreadsheet of all 145 articles (DOI, method, sample size, theories, etc.) "cannot be shown here, but it is available upon request" (p. 1444). The Data Availability statement repeats this (p. 1467). Contact: elia.pizzolitto@unich.it (p. 1439). The reference list covers only the roughly 100 works cited in the text, not all 145. So the full list may hold more relevant papers than these notes can surface.

### Search funnel (p. 1443, Fig. 1)
- **Databases:** SCOPUS, EBSCOHost, Web of Science.
- **Keywords:** `music AND (business OR management)` in title, abstract and keywords.
- **SCOPUS:** 7,825 hits, then 3,764 (English, peer-reviewed), then 1,333 (2017–2022).
- **EBSCOHost:** 17,765 hits, then 183.
- **Web of Science:** 2,944 hits, then 855.
- **Pooled and screened:** 2,371 pooled, 771 duplicates removed, 1,444 false positives removed, leaving **145**.
  - Arithmetic note: the text says 1,444 false positives but Fig. 1 says 1,455. Only 1,455 sums to 145. Trivial.

### Descriptive stats (pp. 1446–1448)
- **Countries:** UK and USA together produced 28.96% of articles. By author count (Fig. 3), the UK leads with about 57 and the USA has about 38.
- **Methods:** case studies were 26.42% of the dataset and interviews 18.87%. Surveys were about 10 papers (Fig. 5).
- **The field is mostly qualitative.** Large-sample quantitative or survey evidence is scarce. **[Inference]** That lowers the odds this literature holds prevalence numbers (B, C, E).
- **Paper types:** empirical papers dominate every year. 2022 is a partial year (12 empirical, 1 theoretical; Fig. 4).

### The emergent themes: "four" vs three
The paper's description and its figures disagree on the number of themes.
- **Text:** p. 1444 says "four themes emerged."
- **Figures and sections:** Fig. 6 (pp. 1448–1449) and Section 4 present **three** top-level themes with **nine** subthemes.
- **Likely fourth theme [Inference]:** "Effects of background music" (4.1.4) is drawn as a subtheme of Theme 1 but is topically separate. It is probably the "fourth theme" the text means.
- The abstract lists the findings as:
  1. digital revolution effects plus the persisting oligopoly;
  2. the renaissance of live music;
  3. segmentation in festivals and the limited priority of sustainability;
  4. musicians' identity.

**Theme 1: "Digital revolution and music industry: have things actually changed?" (4.1, pp. 1450–1455)**
- **4.1.1 Effects of the digital revolution (pp. 1450–1452).**
  - Productivity and competitiveness gains, plus "digital dark matter" (Vendrell-Herrero et al. 2017).
  - Three waves of disruptive innovation: digital distribution, downloads, streaming (Urbinati et al. 2019).
  - Supply chain evolved from physical (1980s) to transitional (1990s) to digital ecosystem (Nakano & Fleury 2017).
  - Psychological ownership under access-based streaming (Sinclair & Tinson 2017).
  - Co-innovation (Saragih et al. 2019a, b).
- **4.1.2 Persisting oligopoly (pp. 1452–1453).**
  - The "celestial jukebox" did not democratize the market. Self-released musicians stay marginal (Sun 2019; Qu et al. 2021).
  - Superstar effect persists (Coelho & Mendes 2019).
  - Spotify shows a bias toward major-label content (Prey et al. 2020).
  - Majors negotiate directly with platforms, while "small labels must depend on intermediaries" (p. 1453; Dellyana et al. 2017; Sun 2019).
- **4.1.3 Social networks and streaming (pp. 1453–1454).**
  - Direct artist-fan communication without intermediaries (Ansari et al. 2018).
  - Reward-based crowdfunding helps, but "problems can arise due to young fans' apprehension" (p. 1454; Gamble et al. 2017).
  - Streaming has been the largest revenue source since 2015 (Arditi 2017).
  - Musicians are "losing control of the revenues" (Towse 2020). In Norway, copyright revenue is about 20% of musicians' earnings, "Therefore, the solution should be designed around live performance revenues" (p. 1454).
- **4.1.4 Background music (p. 1455).** Retail and workplace effects of music (Michel et al. 2017; Landay & Harms 2019). Not relevant to OMP.

**Theme 2: "Live music events and performance: a renovated and segmentate phenomenon" (4.2, pp. 1456–1458)**
- **4.2.1 Renewed consumer interest in live performance (p. 1456).**
  - Record success correlates positively with concert success. Quality of music correlates only weakly with fame (Papies & van Heerde 2017).
  - Classical music is in crisis: consumers say they lack time for classical concerts (Pompe et al. 2017).
  - During Covid, musicians ran fully online events (Areiza-Padilla & Galindo-Becerra 2022).
- **4.2.2 Experience and segmentation in festivals (pp. 1456–1457).**
  - Four experience dimensions: aesthetics, entertainment, escape, education (Aşan et al. 2020).
  - Festivals are "complex, spontaneous" (Laberschek et al. 2020). Segmentation is context-dependent (Kim & Kang 2022).
  - Segmentation studies: Finnish festivals (Kinnunen et al. 2018), a military music festival (Mallette et al. 2018), live opera (Roll et al. 2014), a benefit concert using psychographic segmentation (Kruger & Viljoen 2021).
  - Innovation implementation needs harmony and functionality (Li et al. 2017).
- **4.2.3 Sustainability in festival management (pp. 1457–1458).**
  - 14 best practices from 10 international events (Wickham et al. 2021).
  - Festival benefits are overestimated (Han et al. 2017).
  - **64% of the Canadian festivals sampled did not communicate their sustainability practices, and only 6% campaigned on social media** (Dodds et al. 2020, p. 1458).
  - Co-creation attendee types (Werner et al. 2019). Stakeholder collaboration (Hazel & Mason 2020; Richardson 2018).

**Theme 3: "Musicians: identity and business model in the digital era" (4.3, pp. 1458–1460)**
- **4.3.1 Musicians' identity (pp. 1458–1459).**
  - Identity is split between a bohemian side and an entrepreneurial side. The split is weaker in young musicians. Open-mindedness and career-mindedness bridge it (Schediwy et al. 2018).
  - The art-vs-business dilemma (Pizzolitto 2021).
  - In the UK and Netherlands, digitalization "will probably reduce" independent musicians' opportunities (Everts & Haynes 2021).
  - Turkey: commodification makes career-building "an illusion" (Güven 2020).
- **4.3.2 Changing business models (pp. 1459–1460).**
  - Decline of studio work (Herbst & Albrecht 2018).
  - Music sits in a portfolio of services that musicians manage themselves (Eiriz & Leite 2017). Career goals are unchanged but the route has changed (Schwetter 2016).
  - Government/NGO cooperation (Ibrahimova 2019). Rapper collaboration (Carter & Welsh 2018).
  - Roles are blurred across musicians, producers and labels (Walzer 2016).
  - **Everts et al. (2021): early-career Dutch musicians "still devote most of their time to creative work". They do not spend time boosting social-network position because "they perceive that the funds needed to flourish in that environment are becoming prohibitive". They build fan bases "outside the internet" and "enjoy the entrepreneurial part" (p. 1460).**
  - Copyright law favors majors (Schroff & Street 2017).

## 2. Question-by-question (A–J)

### A. How US music educators and community ensembles handle ticketing, RSVPs and cash today; fee and cash-handling pain
- **Paper says:** nothing.
  - Music education appears only as a field the review excludes: earlier mapping work is cited (p. 1441), and Boyakova 2018 was excluded as non-business (p. 1444).
  - "Teaching and other educational activities" is named as a dimension of the music industry (p. 1440, citing Thomson 2013).
  - Community ensembles, schools, booster clubs and RSVPs are not mentioned anywhere.
- **Relevant cited works (all tangential):**
  - **Pompe, Tamburri & Munn (2017),** "Subscription ticket sales for symphony orchestras: are flexible subscription tickets sustainable?" *Managerial and Decision Economics* 39(1):71–78. The only ticketing-titled paper in the references. It concerns nonprofit orchestras (US, by the look of the authors and journal **[Inference]**). It may hint at how nonprofit ensembles price and sell tickets. It does not cover school or community groups.
  - **Henry (2013),** "The musical experiences, career aspirations, and attitudes toward the music education profession of all-state musicians," *Journal of Music Teacher Education* 24:40–53. US music-ed context. It is about career aspirations, not operations.
  - **Thomson (2013),** "Roles, revenue, and responsibilities: the changing nature of being a working musician," *Work and Occupations* 40(4):514–525. Covers the teaching and gig portfolio. No ticketing.
  - **Kullak, Baker & Woratschek (2021),** "Enhancing value creation in social purpose organizations: business models that leverage networks," *Journal of Business Research* 125:630–642. Nonprofit and social-purpose business models. Cited p. 1466 for the claim that managers of minor organizations should coordinate networks with access to resources. **[Inference]** A possible frame for community ensembles as social-purpose organizations.
  - Welch et al. (2004) *Psychology of Music* 32:239–290; Gruhn (2004) *Psychology of Music* 32:311–321. These map music-education *research* in the UK and Germany. They are not about operations.
- **Gap:** total. Look to music-education trade sources and survey instruments: NAfME, state MEAs, Chorus America, the League of American Orchestras, Association of Concert Bands, and booster-club and school-district finance policies. Direct educator surveys are another source. **[Inference]**

### B. Share of small shows that are door-only/cash-only, under 300 capacity, or free
- **Paper says:** nothing. No venue-size, door-sales or payment-method data.
- **Relevant cited works:**
  - **Azzellini, Greer & Umney (2021),** "Why isn't there an Uber for live music? The digitalisation of intermediaries and the limits of the platform economy," *New Technology, Work and Employment* 37(1):1–23. Cited p. 1440. **[Inference from title]** It probably documents informal, relationship-based booking and payment in live music. That is the best lead in this paper for door-deal and cash practices, though likely UK or European rather than US.
  - **Papies & van Heerde (2017),** "The dynamic interplay between recorded music and live concerts: the role of piracy, unbundling, and artist characteristics," *Journal of Marketing* 81:67–87. Concert data, but at artist and tour level, not small venues.
- **Gap:** total. This is venue-economics and grey-literature territory: NIVA, city music-census reports (e.g. Austin, Seattle, London Music Venue Trust), and Music Venue Trust annual reports. **[Inference]**

### C. Sizing non-consumption: counts of K-12/college concerts, community bands, church concerts, house concerts
- **Paper says:** nothing. The only market sizing is global recorded-music revenue from IFPI 2022 (p. 1440).
- **Relevant cited works:** none that size US amateur or community activity. Pompe et al. (2017) is about classical concert attendance decline (p. 1456), not counts.
- **Gap:** total. Look at the NEA Survey of Public Participation in the Arts, NCES/Fast Response Survey on arts education, the Chorus America "Chorus Impact" study, Association of Concert Bands membership, the Hartford Institute (congregations), and house-concert networks (Concerts in Your Home). **[Inference]**

### D. Small venues' dependence on ticketing-fee rebates; share of fee income retained
- **Paper says:** nothing on ticketing fees, rebates or venue P&L. Pricing discussion is limited to streaming (advertising intensity, subscription fees, song prices; Li et al. 2020, p. 1454).
- **Relevant cited works (tangential):**
  - Pompe et al. (2017): ticket pricing and subscription design for orchestras.
  - **Li, Luo, Qiu & Bandyopadhyay (2020),** "Optimal pricing model of digital music: subscription, ownership or mixed?" *Production and Operations Management* 29:688–704. Streaming, not tickets.
  - Azzellini et al. (2021): intermediaries in live music. **[Inference]** It may touch on intermediary take rates.
- **Gap:** total. Sources would be US antitrust record (DOJ v. Live Nation/Ticketmaster complaint, 2024), FTC junk-fee proceedings, Senate hearing testimony (Jan 2023), and NIVA statements. **[Inference]**

### E. Frequency of opaque, late or missing settlement for independent artists at small venues
- **Paper says:** no settlement data. It does give general context:
  - Musicians face "instability of income, uncertainty, and exploitative tendencies in the market" (p. 1458, discussing Schediwy et al. 2018).
  - Musicians are "losing control of the revenues that come from their music" (p. 1454, Towse 2020). The Norway copyright figure means about 80% of earnings come from non-copyright sources, presumably mostly live. That makes live income, and so settlement, central to musicians' livelihoods. **[Inference, using the paper's own numbers]**
  - Concerts are "launching pads" for less famous artists (p. 1467, Papies & van Heerde 2017).
- **Relevant cited works:**
  - **Azzellini et al. (2021)** (above). The best lead on live-music labor and intermediary practices.
  - **Towse (2020),** "Dealing with digital: the economic organisation of streamed music," *Media, Culture & Society* 42(7–8):1461–1478. Source of the revenue-control argument.
  - **Herbst & Albrecht (2018),** "The work realities of professional studio musicians in the German popular music recording industry: careers, practices and economic situations," *IASPM Journal* 8:18–37. Musicians' economic conditions (studio, not live).
  - **Everts, Hitters & Berkers (2021),** "The working life of musicians: mapping the work activities and values of early-career pop musicians in the Dutch music industry," *Creative Industries Journal* 15:97–117. Time-use and work activities. Possibly includes gig admin.
  - **Güven (2020),** "Hoping for the best, preparing for the worst: a sociological study on being a musician in Turkey," *Journal of Economy Culture and Society*. Precarity.
  - **Thomson (2013)** (above). Revenue portfolio.
  - **Haynes & Marshall (2017),** "Reluctant entrepreneurs: musicians and entrepreneurship in the 'new' music industry," *British Journal of Sociology* 69:459–482.
- **Gap:** no prevalence figures. The MEAA figure already found remains the only quantitative anchor. Other places to look: the Musicians' Union (UK) "Fair Play" venue work, the American Federation of Musicians, the Future of Music Coalition "Artist Revenue Streams" survey (2012), and the Music Workers Alliance. **[Inference]**

### F. Whether artists get buyer/fan data from ticketing platforms; cost of data lock-in
- **Paper says:** nothing on ticketing data access. Related points:
  - Managers should be "collecting as much information as possible on the changing nature of their audiences" (p. 1466, citing Loureiro et al. 2021).
  - Hire community managers who run social research on participants' preferences (p. 1466, Llopis-Amorós et al. 2018).
  - Develop "technological tools to allow for the establishment and maintenance of a relationship between distributors and consumers" (p. 1466, Tran et al. 2018).
  - Social networks let artist-fan communication bypass intermediaries (p. 1453, Ansari et al. 2018).
  - Platform power: Spotify playlists reduce Spotify's dependence on majors, and Spotify shows major-label bias (p. 1453, Prey et al. 2020).
- **Relevant cited works:**
  - **Ansari, Stahl, Heitmann & Bremer (2018),** "Building a social network for success," *Journal of Marketing Research* 55:321–338. Artists' own networks as an asset.
  - **Prey, Esteve Del Valle & Zwerwer (2020),** "Platform pop: disentangling Spotify's intermediary role in the music industry," *Information, Communication & Society* 25(1):74–92. Platform intermediary power. A conceptual analogue for ticketing-platform lock-in. **[Inference]**
  - **Llopis-Amorós, Gil-Saura & Ruiz Molina (2018),** "The role of marketing communications in generating brand equity for an event," *Event Management* 22:825–848.
  - **Loureiro, Silva & Sarmento (2021),** "Exploring emotional states and surprise consumption as factors of involvement in music festivals," *Revista Turismo & Desenvolvimento* 36(2):81–89.
  - **Tran, Goulding & Shiu (2018),** "The orchestra of ideas: using music to enhance the 'fuzzy front end' phase of product innovation," *Journal of Business Research* 85:504–513. Cited for the distributor-consumer relationship-tools claim. **[Inference from title]** Its actual content looks like a weak fit for that claim.
  - **Choi & Burnes (2017),** "Bonding and spreading," *Management Decision* 55:1905–1923. Social media and fan engagement.
  - **Gamble, McAdam & Brennan (2018),** "How user-centric innovation is affecting stakeholder marketing strategies: exploratory findings from the music industry," *European Management Review* 16:1175–1193.
- **Gap:** no evidence on ticketing data-sharing terms or lock-in costs. Sources would be platform terms of service, the DOJ v. Live Nation complaint (exclusive contracts and data), artist-manager surveys (Music Managers Forum), and Bandsintown/Songkick industry statements. **[Inference]**

### G. Fan behavior with crypto wallets, NFT tickets or decentralized platforms
- **Paper says:** nothing. Blockchain, NFT, crypto, Web3 and decentralization do not appear in the text or references. The search likely ran around early 2022, given submission in March 2022, so 2022 coverage is partial.

  - **Sinclair & Tinson (2017),** "Psychological ownership and music streaming consumption," *Journal of Business Research* 71:1–9. Consumers rebuild a sense of ownership under access models (p. 1451). Relevant to fan attitudes to ownership, not data ownership. **[Inference]**
  - **Naveed, Watanabe & Neittaanmäki (2017),** "Co-evolution between streaming and live music leads a way to the sustainable growth of music industry," *Technology in Society* 50:1–19. Consumers show "more propensity to acquire digital property" (p. 1456).
  - **Gamble, Brennan & McAdam (2017),** "A rewarding experience? Exploring how crowdfunding is affecting music industry business models," *Journal of Business Research* 70:25–36.   - **Arbatani, Asadi & Omidi (2018),** "Media innovations in digital music distribution: the case of beeptunes.com," in *Competitiveness in Emerging Markets* (Springer), pp. 93–108. An alternative platform case study.

### H. Musician identity, entrepreneurship, willingness to adopt business tools
- **Paper says:** a lot. This is Theme 3 (pp. 1458–1460) plus the identity rows in Table 1.
  - Musicians are reluctant to see themselves as entrepreneurs because of the art-profit tension (p. 1441, Pizzolitto 2021).
  - The lack of formal credentials is a barrier to music being seen as a profession (p. 1441, Frederickson & Rooney 1990).
  - The split identity is weaker in young musicians. Open-mindedness and career-mindedness bridge it (pp. 1458–1459).
  - Early-career musicians spend most time on creative work. They avoid investing in social-media positioning because the required funds seem prohibitive. They build fan bases offline. Yet they "enjoy the entrepreneurial part" (p. 1460, Everts et al. 2021).
  - Independent musicians increasingly do label and agency work themselves through self-publishing and self-management (p. 1460, Schwetter 2016; Eiriz & Leite 2017).
  - The paper recommends the psychological theory of professional identity (Marcia 1966) to study what lets musicians accept entrepreneurship (p. 1465).
  - **[Inference for OMP]** A settlement tool is more likely adopted if it costs no money and very little time, sits next to the gig (not "business admin"), and is framed as protecting the creative work rather than as entrepreneurship. Younger musicians are the likelier early adopters. None of this is tested in the paper.
- **Relevant cited works:**
  - **Schediwy, Bhansing & Loots (2018),** "Young musicians' career identities: do bohemian and entrepreneurial career identities compete or cohere?" *Creative Industries Journal* 11:174–196.
  - **Pizzolitto (2021),** "Are musicians entrepreneurs? A preliminary analysis," 7th Int'l Scientific-Business Conference LIMEN 2021. Conference paper by the same author.
  - **Everts & Haynes (2021),** "Taking care of business: the routines and rationales of early-career musicians in the Dutch and British music industries," *International Journal of Cultural Studies* 24(5):731–748. **Most directly relevant to business-routine adoption.** **[Inference from title]**
  - **Everts, Hitters & Berkers (2021)** (above, under E).
  - **Haynes & Marshall (2017),** "Reluctant entrepreneurs" (above, under E).
  - **Bridson, Evans, Varman, Volkov & McDonald (2017),** "Questioning worth: selling out in the music industry," *European Journal of Marketing* 51:1650–1668. Authenticity costs of commercial moves.
  - **Schwetter (2016),** "From record contract to artrepreneur? Musicians' self-management and the changing illusio in the music market," *Kritika Kultura* 32:183–207.
  - **Eiriz & Leite (2017),** "The digital distribution of music and its impact on the business models of independent musicians," *Service Industries Journal* 37(13–14):875–895.
  - **Walzer (2016),** "Independent music production: how individuality, technology and creative entrepreneurship influence contemporary music industry practices," *Creative Industries Journal* 10(1):21–39.
  - **Morrow (2018),** "Distributed agility: artist co-management in the music attention economy," *International Journal of Arts Management* 20:38–48. Shared management teams. Relevant to who would actually use a settlement tool (managers vs artists). **[Inference]**
  - **Carter & Welsh (2018),** "'Everybody wants to work with me': collaborative labor in hip hop," *Popular Music and Society* 42(3):267–283.
  - **Herbst & Albrecht (2018)**; **Güven (2020)**; **Thomson (2013)** (above).
  - **Frederickson & Rooney (1990),** "How the music occupation failed to become a profession," *International Review of the Aesthetics and Sociology of Music* 21:189.
  - **Harbor (2020),** "The marketing of concerts in London 1672–1749," *Journal of Historical Research in Marketing* 12:449–471. Historical concert marketing and promotion.
  - **Marcia (1966),** ego-identity status, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 3(5):551–558. Theory only.
- **Gap:** no study measures musicians' adoption of specific business software or financial tools. The evidence is qualitative and mostly UK, Netherlands and Europe (Fig. 3 and the cited works). US-specific evidence would need other sources. **[Inference]**

### I. Festival/event management: volunteer-run events, cash handling, ticketing, audience data

Agenda points from the prose:
- More single case studies on what lets minor artists emerge without major-label resources. Research on reducing "barriers that traditionally impede the emergence of minor artists and labels" (p. 1463, citing Pillai et al. 2021; Walzer 2016). Researchers "should help minor labels and artists find better applications of existing technologies characterized by low costs" (p. 1463). **[Inference]** This is the clearest academic statement of OMP's thesis.

**Not on the agenda (inference):** ticketing economics, settlement, payment practices, fan-data ownership, decentralized platforms, amateur, school or community music. The field has not flagged these as open questions because it has not engaged with them.

## 4. Gaps: what the paper's scope excludes

The paper states these exclusions itself:
- **Time window 2017–2022**, with 2022 partial (p. 1443; Fig. 4).
- **English-language, peer-reviewed journal articles only**, from SCOPUS, EBSCOHost and WoS (pp. 1442–1443).
- **Books, book chapters and grey literature are excluded** (p. 1446, limitation 2). Industry reports, trade surveys, government data and think-tank work are therefore out of scope, and that is where most of A–G's evidence would be. **[Inference]**
- **Single-author coding** (p. 1446, limitation 1).
- **Narrow keyword string** `music AND (business OR management)` (p. 1442). Papers framed as "ticketing," "concert," "live music economy," "arts administration," "music education," "nonprofit," or "platform" without those words would be missed. **[Inference]**
- **Must address business/management.** Pure music-education papers were excluded (p. 1444, Boyakova example).

Observed absences (I checked the full text and reference list):
- No ticketing-fee, secondary-market, dynamic-pricing or ticket-platform studies. The only ticketing title is Pompe et al. 2017, on orchestra subscriptions.
- No US small-venue, grassroots-venue or venue-economics studies.
- No settlement, contracts, booking-agent or promoter-deal studies.
- No school, college, community, church or house-concert music.
- No blockchain, NFT, crypto or Web3.
- No cooperatives, commons or open source.
- No volunteer-management or cash-handling studies.
- The geography leans UK, Europe and Asia. The UK leads author counts; there is US authorship but few US-context live-music studies in the cited set.

**Where to look instead [Inference, not from the paper]:**
- **Grey literature and data:** NEA SPPA; NIVA; Music Venue Trust (UK); city music censuses; Future of Music Coalition; Chorus America; League of American Orchestras; NAfME; Pollstar/IQ small-venue data; DOJ v. Live Nation (2024) filings; MEAA (already found).
- **Journals for targeted searches:** *International Journal of Event and Festival Management*; *Event Management*; *International Journal of Arts Management*; *Journal of Arts Management, Law, and Society*; *Arts Education Policy Review*; *Journal of Music Teacher Education*; *Popular Music and Society*; *Creative Industries Journal*; *New Technology, Work and Employment*.
- **Post-2022 work:** NFT ticketing and Web3 music, and live-music recovery after Covid.
