# Technical, Legal and Procurement Literature for Open Music Performance (OMP)

Research date: 2026-10-15. Method: web search plus fetches of selected primary pages. Most academic items were checked by abstract, publisher listing, or NBER/SSRN/RePEc page; full texts were generally **not** opened. Each bullet says how it was verified. "Not found" means a targeted search returned nothing usable, not that the material cannot exist.

Legend: **[abstract]** = publisher/NBER/SSRN/arXiv abstract or listing seen; **[fetched]** = primary page opened and read; **[secondary]** = known only through a summary, news report, or law-firm memo; **[not opened]** = cited from prior knowledge, existence not re-confirmed this session.

## 1. Ticketing economics literature

### Takeaway
The economics literature firmly establishes that primary tickets are systematically underpriced, that the gap feeds resale and rent-seeking, and that hiding fees until checkout raises spending. It says almost nothing about fees at small venues or school/community events. Exclusivity is documented mainly in the DOJ/states complaint and the April 2026 jury verdict, not in peer-reviewed economics. OMP can cite the literature on price salience and underpricing; any claim about fees at small venues has to rest on OMP's own data or trade sources.

### Cited Findings
**Underpricing, resale, rent-seeking**
- Leslie & Sorensen, "Resale and Rent-Seeking: An Application to Ticket Markets," *Review of Economic Studies* 81 (2014): 266–300, doi:10.1093/restud/rdt033. Equilibrium model on matched primary and secondary data for major rock concerts. Resale raises allocative efficiency by about 5%, and roughly one-third of that gain is lost to rent-seeking and resale transaction costs. The 2010 working-paper version said half, so cite the published one-third figure. [abstract] — [Bocconi abstract](https://economics.unibocconi.eu/sites/default/files/files/media/attachments/abstract20110705092445.pdf); [NBER SI 2008 draft](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2008/si2008/IO/leslie.pdf)
- Budish & Bhave, "Primary-Market Auctions for Event Tickets: Eliminating the Rents of 'Bob the Broker'?" *AEJ: Microeconomics* 15(3) (2023): 142–170, doi:10.1257/mic.20180230 (NBER WP 23770, 2017). Uses Ticketmaster's mid-2000s primary auctions plus eBay resale data. Auctions improved price discovery, roughly doubled performer revenue, and nearly eliminated broker arbitrage on average. Auctions still failed to take off, which the paper discusses. [abstract] — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23770); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3035124); [replication data](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/161484/view)
- Courty, "Ticket resale, bots, and the fair price ticketing curse," *Journal of Cultural Economics* 43(3) (2019): 345–363, doi:10.1007/s10824-019-09353-4. Organizers underprice to keep resellers from profiting (the "curse"), and online ticketing makes this worse. Courty argues that no current public or private remedy works. He proposes a name-tied primary lottery with tickets checked at admission and returns redistributed at face value or below. [abstract/secondary] — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jculte/v43y2019i3d10.1007_s10824-019-09353-4.html); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10824-019-09353-4); [Culture.Case summary 2025](https://culturecase.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/research/2025/07/use-centralised-exchange-beat-ticket-scalpers)
- Courty, "Some Economics of Ticket Resale," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 17(2) (2003): 85–97. This is the standard survey of why tickets are underpriced and how resale works. [not opened]
- Connolly & Krueger, "Rockonomics: The Economics of Popular Music," NBER WP 11282 (2005), published in *Handbook of the Economics of Art and Culture* vol. 1 (Elsevier, 2006): 667–719. Concerts are the main source of performer income, ticket prices rose much faster than CPI, and revenue is highly concentrated among superstars. A secondary source reports Krueger's estimate that underpricing one 2002 Springsteen show forfeited about $4M. [abstract; Springsteen figure secondary] — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/11282.html); [Princeton 2002 news](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2002/09/24/economist-alan-krueger-examines-pricing-concert-tickets)
- Krueger, *Rockonomics: A Backstage Tour of What the Music Industry Can Teach Us about Economics and Life* (Currency/Crown, 2019). A trade book covering superstar economics and the fairness norms behind underpricing. [not opened]
- Mortimer, Nosko & Sorensen, "Supply Responses to Digital Distribution: Recorded Music and Live Performances," *Information Economics and Policy* 24(1) (2012): 3–14 (NBER WP 16507). Panel of about 2,000 artists. File-sharing cut album sales but raised concert demand, and the effect was **strongest for smaller acts**. This is the closest the literature comes to small-artist live economics. [abstract] — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16507); [Copyright Evidence wiki](https://www.copyrightevidence.org/evidence-wiki/index.php/Mortimer,_Nosko_and_Sorensen_(2012))
- Waddell, Barnet & Berry, *This Business of Concert Promotion and Touring* (Billboard Books, 2007). This is the practitioner reference on promoter, venue, and ticketer deal structure, including rebates. It is industry description, not empirical research. [not opened]

**Fees and price salience**
- Blake, Moshary, Sweeney & Tadelis, "Price Salience and Product Choice," *Marketing Science* (2021) (NBER WP 25186, 2018). Field experiment from StubHub's switch to all-in pricing, where fees ran about 15% plus shipping. Showing fees upfront reduced both purchases and the quality of seats chosen, and the quality effect explains at least 28% of the revenue decline. Secondary coverage adds that users who saw fees late spent about 21% more and were 14% more likely to buy; check those two numbers against the paper. [abstract; 21%/14% secondary] — [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186.pdf); [Haas PDF](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/stadelis/AIP.pdf); [Ticketing Business coverage](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2021/02/19/drip-pricing-leads-to-consumers-spending-more-on-tickets-study/)
- FTC "Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees" (approved Dec 17, 2024; effective May 12, 2025, though some sources say mid-April). Live-event ticketing must show the total price upfront. Fee levels are not capped, and taxes and shipping can be shown separately. [secondary] — [Mesa Arts Center](https://www.mesaartscenter.com/ticket-pricing-worksheet-frc); [NBC/CNBC](https://www.nbcboston.com/news/business/money-report/the-ftcs-new-rule-on-ticket-prices-is-a-win-for-consumers-experts-say-but-dont-expect-it-to-bring-costs-down/3711940/?amp=1)
- Effects after the rule: Live Nation said all-in pricing raised sales about 8% (company claim). A Guardian report, seen only through an aggregator, says Ticketmaster amended contracts at at least 8 public venues to raise service fees after the rule took effect. Both are **unverified**. — [Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/?p=1201103)

**Exclusivity and the DOJ/states case**
- DOJ and states complaint (May 2024). It alleges Ticketmaster's exclusive contracts cover more than 75% of concert ticket sales at major concert venues and that they lock venues in for many years with upfront signing payments and sponsorships. These are allegations, not findings. [primary, not opened in full] — [DOJ complaint](https://www.justice.gov/atr/media/1353101/dl)
- DOJ settled mid-trial in March 2026 without divesting Ticketmaster. Reported terms: exclusivity capped at 4 years, a non-exclusive option for venues, rival ticketers listed on Ticketmaster, and a 15% fee cap at covered venues. Many states rejected the settlement and kept litigating. [secondary] — [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/live-nation-settlement-need-to-know-next-1235528047/); [Harvard Gazette, Mar 2026](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/03/for-now-live-nation-deal-is-just-a-band-aid-says-antitrust-scholar/)
- **Jury verdict, April 15, 2026 (S.D.N.Y.).** Live Nation and Ticketmaster were found liable on all counts, including monopolization of primary ticketing and illegal tying of promotion and venues. Damages were set at $1.72 per primary concert ticket. Post-trial motions were set for July 31, 2026, with a remedies phase still pending. I did not confirm the current status. [primary + secondary] — [NY AG press release](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-coalition-states-win-trial-against-live-nation-and); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/15/nx-s1-5786715/live-nation-ticketmaster-antitrust-verdict-monopoly); [Live Nation 10-Q Q2 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001335258/000133525826000035/lyv-20260630.htm); [Paul Weiss memo](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/live-nationticketmaster-antitrust-verdict-key-takeaways-from-the-states-jury-trial-win)
- Non-peer-reviewed work on exclusivity: a Yale Thurman Arnold Project student paper (2025) on foreclosure through venue contracts, and a 2024 *Journal of Business and Economic Development* article with HHI estimates (low-tier journal, Science Publishing Group). — [Yale TAP 2025](https://som.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/4.%20Ticket%20Master.pdf); [JBED 2024](https://www.sciencepublishinggroup.com/article/10.11648/j.jbed.20240904.12)

### Inferences
- OMP can cite Blake et al. (2021) for transparent, all-in, low-fee pricing, and Courty (2019) for name-tied, face-value, exchange-at-par tickets, which fits a nonprofit or school-concert framing.
- Mortimer, Nosko & Sorensen (2012) is the only peer-reviewed result I found that singles out smaller acts in live music.
- For exclusivity, the strongest citable sources are now court and party documents (the complaint, the verdict, and the 10-Q), not economics papers. The settlement's exclusivity cap and non-exclusive option, if a court adopts them as remedies, would mainly affect large venues, not schools.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** any peer-reviewed study of ticketing fees at small, independent, school, or community venues, or of fee incidence at the low end of the market.
- **Not found:** a peer-reviewed economics paper (2024–2026) analyzing ticketing exclusivity or the DOJ case. Only student papers and law-firm memos turned up.
- **Not found:** independent measurement of what the FTC all-in rule did to fees.
- I did not confirm the post-trial ruling or remedies status after July 31, 2026.

## 2. Offline-verifiable tickets and check-in

### Takeaway
Signed tickets checked offline are well-established practice: rail (UIC 918-3/FCB), pretix's EdDSA "signature scheme 1," and Zupass/PCD tickets at Devconnect. Every source handles **double entry** one of two ways: accept that offline scanners cannot prevent it in real time and reconcile afterward, or put a local list of used or valid tickets on the scanner or a LAN proxy. I found no standard or peer-reviewed scheme that prevents double entry across independent offline scanners. The academic e-ticket literature *detects* double use after the fact, usually by de-anonymizing the reuser.

### Cited Findings
- **pretix ticket secrets.** "pretix signature scheme 1" encodes product, variation, and date in the ticket code and signs it with **EdDSA**, so validation needs neither a server connection nor a ticket database on the scanner. pretix calls it an advanced option, intended mainly for events over 25,000 tickets, and says offline scanning with it "is much more limited." The default is to download the full ticket database to each scanner. [secondary of docs] — [pretix docs: ticket secrets](https://docs.pretix.eu/guides/ticket-secrets/); [older docs](https://docs.pretix.eu/en/latest/user/events/ticket_secrets.html)
- **pretixSCAN Proxy** is a LAN server that stands in for on-device storage in offline mode, so scanners share one local state. This is the venue-local way to stop double entry. [secondary of docs] — [pretix docs / ecosyste.ms listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fpretix%2Fpretixscan-proxy); [pretixSCAN Android guide](https://docs.pretix.eu/guides/pretixscan/android/)
- **pretix check-in algorithm.** The developer doc says offline verification uses one of two approaches, depending on whether the local database holds every ticket, implemented in `libpretixsync`. Server-side deduplication uses a `nonce` so the same scan isn't uploaded twice. The page does **not** describe conflict resolution between two offline scanners. [fetched] — [pretix dev docs: check-in algorithm](https://docs.pretix.eu/dev/_sources/development/algorithms/checkin.rst.txt)
- **UIC rail barcode standards.** UIC leaflet 918-3 defines a signed barcode header. The Flexible Content Barcode (FCB, ASN.1/PER encoded) is registered under IRS 90918-9. A UIC newsletter says the old 918-3 header cannot carry stronger signatures, which motivated the newer DOSIPAS dynamic header. Open-source decoders verify 918-3 signatures against a public-key infrastructure. A Dutch metro case study says gates decide entry from data in the barcode, with offline checks kept as a fallback. [abstract/secondary; spec itself paywalled in UIC shop] — [UIC FCB page](https://css0.uic.org/passenger/passenger-services-group/article/fcb-flexible-content-barcode); [UIC IRS 90918-9 shop](https://shop.uic.org/en/14274-digital-security-elements-for-rail-passenger-ticketing-9793.html); [UIC e-news 2020](https://css0.uic.org/com/enews/nr/681/article/the-uic-passenger-technical-groups-on-ticket-layout-ticket-security-and); [Ximedes TapCheck](https://blog.ximedes.com/2020-09-26/tapcheck-production-ready/); [uic-918-3 npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/uic-918-3); [ERA TAP B.12](https://www.era.europa.eu/system/files/2023-10/ERA-OPI-2023-4%20-%20ERA_Technical_Document_TAP_B_12.pdf)
- **Zupass / PCD (0xPARC).** Zupass is a local-first wallet for proof-carrying data. An organizer-signed ticket PCD can be verified by third parties, and holders can prove attendance without revealing identity. Devcon DIP-31 proposed replacing pretix with PCD-based ticketing at Devconnect. A later DIP (POD-based ticketing at Devcon) builds on it, and new tickets use `PODTicketPCD`. Reports differ on how successful it was ("experiment" vs "success"). [secondary of forum/docs] — [Devcon forum DIP-31](https://forum.devcon.org/t/dip-regarding-pcd-based-ticketing-for-devconnect/3201); [DIP-46 POD ticketing](https://forum.devcon.org/t/dip-46-pod-based-ticketing-at-devcon/3850); [PCD docs](https://docs.pcd.team/); [Zupass dev docs](https://0xparc.notion.site/Zupass-Developer-Docs-55da7632b3384a188d270ccdc67bb87c)
- **W3C VC Use Cases (Group Note, 18 March 2026).** It has **no** event-ticket, transit-ticket, or offline-verification use case. The closest items are an air-travel upgrade coupon checked against a status registry, test-center admission, and a non-transferable credential ("Bona-fide Shopper"). Offline verification appears only in threat-model text, and double use is not discussed explicitly. Event tickets as verifiable credentials appear only in vendor material (cheqd demos). [fetched, first 100k of 133k chars] — [W3C VC Use Cases 2026](https://www.w3.org/TR/2026/NOTE-vc-use-cases-20260318/); [cheqd blog](https://cheqd.io/blog/verifiable-credentials-to-streamline-the-events-and-ticketing-industry/)
- **EU Digital Identity Wallet.** A Commission presentation lists "Event Tickets" among expected electronic attestations of attributes. The APTITUDE large-scale pilot has a "Tickets & Check-ins" work package (travel, hotels, tourist sites). The first four large-scale pilots ran 2023–2025. I found **no** event-ticket pilot report or results. [secondary] — [EC Digital Building Blocks pilots](https://ec.europa.eu/digital-building-blocks/sites/spaces/EUDIGITALIDENTITYWALLET/pages/924976339/LSP-POTENTIAL); [ICAO TRIP 2025 slides](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/Meetings/TRIP-Symposium-2025/Presentations/SESSION6/BAUDOUIN.pdf)
- **Peer-reviewed e-ticket cryptography (double use):**
  - "Privacy-Preserving Electronic Ticket Scheme with Attribute-based Credentials" (arXiv 1706.03016; later journal version). Treats over-spend detection as a core requirement. Honest users stay anonymous, and anyone who uses a ticket twice is de-anonymized. [abstract] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03016)
  - Lafourcade et al., "Transferable, Auditable and Anonymous Ticketing Protocol" (ACM AsiaCCS 2024). Its "no-double-spending" property prevents a ticket from being refunded, transferred, or validated twice. Needs a ledger or online component. [abstract] — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3634737.3645008)
  - PriTKT (*Sensors* 24(2), 2024, doi:10.3390/s24020496). Blockchain-recorded validations with zero-knowledge proofs, so double spending is detected and traced. [abstract] — [MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24020496)
  - "An NFC E-ticket system with offline authentication" (circa 2014). Claims offline validation with double-spend security comparable to traceable payments. [listing only; full text not available] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271425694_An_NFC_E-ticket_system_with_offline_authentication)
  - Patents describe validators working offline with allow-lists and deny-lists synced from a server. [abstract] — e.g. [US 10460262](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10460262)
- Regner, Schweizer & Urbach, "NFTs in Practice: Non-Fungible Tokens as Core Component of a Blockchain-based Event Ticketing Application," ICIS 2019 (design-science prototype). Relevant prior art for OMP's later blockchain options. [abstract] — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/icis2019/blockchain_fintech/blockchain_fintech/1/)

### Inferences
- For OMP's design, the prior art points to three layers: (a) venue-signed tickets with Ed25519 (the pretix scheme 1 pattern) give authenticity offline; (b) double entry is prevented only by shared local state, either one scanner or a LAN proxy (the pretixSCAN Proxy pattern); (c) without shared state, double entry is detected when scanners reconcile after reconnecting (the pretix nonce/sync pattern; the cryptography literature's "detect and identify" approach). This matches OMP's v1 assumption. The citations support stating it as a known trade-off, not a new risk.
- Small school venues usually have one or two doors and one or two scanners, which is the setting where single-device or LAN-proxy shared state is cheap.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** any peer-reviewed paper or standard that *prevents* double admission across multiple fully offline, unsynchronized scanners. The reviewed literature consistently treats this as impossible without shared state and settles for detection.
- **Not verified:** how VDV-KA and Calypso handle offline deny-lists for barcodes. My search returned only 2006 VDV chip-card tender documents, and the specs are not openly available.
- **Not opened:** the full UIC 90918-9 / DOSIPAS specification (paywalled). Also not opened: pretix's libpretixsync conflict-handling source and any formal Zupass/PCD paper (only forum posts and docs were found).
- **Not found:** any EUDI Wallet event-ticket pilot results.

## 3. Blockchain ticketing UX and adoption research

### Takeaway
I found **no peer-reviewed study measuring fan adoption, wallet drop-off, or attitudes toward NFT tickets specifically**. Evidence on wallet friction comes from general NFT-purchase surveys and from vendors explaining after the fact why NFT ticketing stalled. The research on decentralized social media is stronger: migrations happen in waves, retention is the hard part, joining a federated network is a known barrier, and users concentrate on a few large servers even when the network is decentralized. That supports OMP's choice to have an operator-hosted default.

### Cited Findings
- Survey of 542 young Korean consumers on NFT purchase intent ("Why do consumers buy NFTs?: Multi-path of attitude and the bandwagon effect"). Higher perceived effort lowered purchase intent, and prior crypto experience predicted purchase because the wallet-funding step was already done. This is general NFT purchasing, not tickets. [listing only; journal and peer review not confirmed] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376714710_Why_do_consumers_buy_NFTs_Multi-path_of_attitude_and_the_bandwagon_effect)
- Security.org survey (n > 1,000 US adults): NFT familiarity rose from 20% to 65% after 2021, but only about 4% of US adults own NFTs. Not peer-reviewed. [secondary] — [Security.org](https://www.security.org/digital-security/nft-market-analysis/)
- DappRadar behavior report: an "access gap," meaning new users need to buy without setting up a wallet or using crypto. Methodology not given. [secondary] — [DappRadar](https://dappradar.com/blog/behavior-report-what-do-consumers-want-from-nfts)
- Industry post-mortem: wallet setup made buying "intimidating and unclear" (vendor blog, no data). — [Wicketsoft](https://www.wicketsoft.com/insights/blog-nft-ticketing/)
- He, Zia, Raman, Castro, Sastry & Tyson, "Flocking to Mastodon: Tracking the Great Twitter Migration," ACM IMC 2023 (arXiv 2302.14294; arXiv lists Zia first). Tracks 136,009 migrating users. The social graph strongly shaped who moved, and users created pressure toward **centralization** on a few large instances inside the decentralized network. [abstract] — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2302.14294?context=cs); [QMUL](https://networks.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/2023/12/11/imc-paper-accepted-2/)
- Balduf et al., "Looking AT the Blue Skies of Bluesky," ACM IMC 2024 (arXiv 2408.12449, doi:10.1145/3646547.3688407). First large-scale measurement of Bluesky/AT Protocol, including how many providers serve each component. The abstract does **not** give the PDS self-hosting share. [fetched abstract] — [arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2408.12449v2); [City open access PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/34428/1/3646547.3688407.pdf)
- "The Rise of Bluesky" (arXiv 2504.12902, 2025, Indiana University). Repeated migration waves produced a stable active user base, but most sign-ups browse briefly and never return, so retention is the central challenge. Preprint. [abstract] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12902v1)
- "Why Academics Are Leaving Twitter for Bluesky" (arXiv 2505.24801, 2025). Information sources help people overcome switching costs. The related literature reports Mastodon's server-choice problem and academics drifting back to Twitter. Preprint. [abstract] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24801v1)

### Inferences
- The Mastodon centralization result (He et al. 2023) and Bluesky's design-level separation (Balduf et al. 2024) both suggest most venues and artists will use an operator default instead of self-hosting. OMP's decision to offer a hosted default with an optional self-host path matches what users have actually done.
- Since no peer-reviewed NFT-ticket adoption data exists, the "no blockchain in v1" decision is best justified by general NFT and wallet friction evidence plus vendor post-mortems, labeled as such.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** peer-reviewed or rigorous industry studies (2021–2026) on NFT-ticket adoption, funnel drop-off, or fan willingness to use crypto wallets for tickets. Vendor claims (GET Protocol, YellowHeart, Ticketmaster on Flow) were not backed by published data.
- **Not found (in abstracts):** the share of Bluesky accounts on non-Bluesky or self-hosted PDSes. The Balduf full text may have it; I did not open it.
- **Not found:** any study of musicians' or venues' attitudes toward decentralized hosting specifically.

## 4. K-12 procurement and student data privacy

### Takeaway
A teacher's click-through use of a hosted tool **is regulated in several key states even when the tool is free**. New York treats click-wrap terms as contracts subject to Ed Law 2-d. Illinois SOPPA requires a written agreement with the district, posted publicly, before an operator receives covered information. California SOPIPA applies to operators whose product is designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes, with or without a contract. Whether OMP is covered depends on (a) whether it is designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes and (b) whether it receives student PII. Parent purchaser emails and student names in concert programs are the likely trigger points. In practice, teachers in NY and IL generally cannot use a click-through tool that touches student data without district sign-off. The usual path is the SDPC NDPA, signed with one district and then extended statewide through each state alliance.

### Cited Findings
**NDPA / SDPC**
- The SDPC (Access 4 Learning Community) maintains the National Data Privacy Agreement as a standard vendor–district template, developed by schools, state organizations, vendors, and lawyers. It covers FERPA and COPPA obligations, and districts add state-specific exhibits. [secondary + primary site] — [privacy.a4l.org](https://privacy.a4l.org/); [Richland One NDPA page](https://www.richlandone.org/departments/information-technology/technology-approval-process/national-data-privacy-agreements); [eduverse SDPC entry](https://edlaw.eduverse.com/docs/jurisdiction/org/sdpc/)
- Signed agreements go into a public registry. In many alliances, a vendor that signs with one district can be adopted by other districts in the same state alliance (Exhibit E "general offer" mechanism, per secondary sources). The Illinois affiliate (LTC Illinois) runs a searchable database of vendors with signed statewide agreements. — [LTC Illinois SDPC](https://www.ltcillinois.org/services/sdpc/); [Runestone Academy blog 2023](https://blog.runestone.academy/2023/05/25/important_privacy_developments.html) (an open-source education project that went through the NDPA; a useful precedent to read in full)
- Scale: an A4L response to a 2022 federal RFI reported 34 participating states, 86 vendors, and about 70,000 signed agreements. The current SDPC site cites about 40 state alliances. Both are dated or self-reported. — [A4L RFI response 2022](https://www.nitrd.gov/rfi/2022/87-fr-35250/Access-4-Learning-Community-PET-RFI-Response-2022.pdf)
- Examples of signed NDPAs with small vendors (Missouri districts) show the template is signed by ordinary small vendors, including an athletics-media vendor. — [Lindbergh–Gipper Media NDPA](https://sdpc.a4l.org/agreements/MO_NDPA_V1%20Lindbergh%20Gipper%20Media-%20Athletic%20Signed.pdf)

**State laws**
- **California SOPIPA (Bus. & Prof. Code §22584).** Applies to operators with actual knowledge that their service is used primarily for K-12 school purposes **and** that was designed and marketed for those purposes. No contract is needed for it to apply. General-audience services are excluded. "K-12 school purposes" includes activities directed by a teacher or school and collaboration among students, staff, or parents. [secondary; statute text not opened] — [FPF SOPIPA At a Glance](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/SOPIPA-At-a-Glance.pdf)
- **New York Ed Law 2-d / 8 NYCRR Part 121.** NYSED's DPO FAQ says click-wrap agreements a user must accept before using a product are subject to 2-d. Contractors must sign the Parents' Bill of Rights, have a data security and privacy plan, and align with the NIST CSF. A 2020 law-firm memo: teachers "will no longer be able to use 'Click through' programs" that collect protected data. [secondary + NYSED FAQ listing] — [NYSED FAQ for DPOs](https://nysed.gov/data-privacy-security/frequently-asked-questions-dpos); [Bond Schoeneck & King memo](https://bsk.com/news-insights/preparing-for-imminent-changes-in-the-new-york-state-education-law-regarding-data-privacy); [Moravia CSD third-party contractor doc](https://www.moraviaschool.org/cms/lib/NY50010813/Centricity/Domain/28//Ed%20Law%202D/Third%20Party%20Contractors.pdf)
- **Illinois SOPPA (105 ILCS 85).** An operator receiving covered information from a district must have a written agreement with required terms (agreements from July 1, 2021 on are void without them). Districts must post operator lists and contracts (within 10 days, per district pages). ISBE publishes a state list annually. "Covered information" is broad. **Accessibility**
- DOJ ADA Title II web rule (April 2024; WCAG 2.1 AA; public schools covered). The standard is unchanged. This applies to the district, and districts pass it to vendors through procurement, typically by asking for a VPAT/ACR. [secondary: law-firm memos] — [Jackson Lewis](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/doj-extends-public-entities-compliance-deadline-ada-related-website-accessibility-hhss-may-2026-deadline-still-looms); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/doj-extends-digital-accessibility-compliance-dates-under-title-ii-of-the-ada/); [Seyfarth ADA Title III blog](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/04/doj-extends-ada-title-ii-website-accessibility-deadlines-for-governmental-entities-but-litigation-and-compliance-risks-remain/)

### Inferences
- **Can a teacher use OMP's hosted instance without district sign-off?** In NY and IL, probably not if OMP stores student PII. Parent and audience purchaser data alone is a grayer area, since it is not clearly "student" data, but a roster of student performers or student-linked accounts would trigger these laws. In CA, SOPIPA applies to OMP if it is marketed for K-12 school purposes, but SOPIPA restricts data use and does not require a contract. Option 1: design v1 so the platform holds **no student PII**, with performers listed only as directory information entered by the teacher, or not at all. Option 2: plan to sign the NDPA, so one signature can spread across a state alliance.
- The Runestone Academy NDPA post suggests a small open-source project can take part in SDPC. Whether a fiscal sponsor can sign on the project's behalf is a question for the sponsor's counsel and SDPC.
- Because of the WCAG 2.1 AA deadlines (2027/2028), an accessibility conformance report (VPAT) will be a routine district request by 2027.

## 5. Money transmission and marketplace platforms

### Takeaway
The strongest available sources favor OMP's model. FinCEN's reasoning is that a party that **never takes possession or control of funds** is not transmitting money, and Stripe's own documentation says that with **direct charges the connected account is the merchant of record**. The agent-of-payee exemption (California §2010(l) and DFPI letters, MTMA, Texas rule) is a *fallback* for platforms that do touch funds. OMP's design avoids needing it as long as it uses direct charges on organizers' own accounts and takes no platform custody. Under the destination or "indirect" charge type, the platform is MoR or bears negative-balance liability, which weakens the position.

### Cited Findings
- **FinCEN FIN-2014-R009 (Aug 27, 2014).** A company acting as an ISO and payment processor is not a money transmitter. The ISO activities don't count because it "never takes possession or control of merchant funds," and the processor activities qualify for the payment-processor exemption (31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii)(B)). The exemption's four conditions: facilitates purchases or bill payment; operates through clearance and settlement systems open only to BSA-regulated institutions; under a formal agreement; and with an agreement at least with the seller receiving the funds. [primary PDF located; summarized via secondary] — [FinCEN FIN-2014-R009](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/administrative_ruling/FIN-2014-R009.pdf); [Orrick summary](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2014-09-05/fincen-rules-regulations-money-services-businesses-do-not-apply-isos-and-exempt-payment-processors/); [Gunster alert](https://gunster.com/alerts/fincen-on-currency-transporters-and-payment-processors/)
- **FIN-2008-R006**, which the brief names: **not located or verified** this session. I make no claim about its content.
- **Stripe Connect, "Understand the merchant of record."** "Direct charges: The merchant of record is the connected account." With indirect charges using `on_behalf_of` the MoR is the connected account, "however, if a connected account's balance becomes negative, your platform is ultimately responsible." Without `on_behalf_of` the MoR is the platform. "For most SaaS platforms, connected accounts are the MoRs; for most marketplaces, the platform is the MoR." [fetched] — [Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/merchant-of-record)
- Stripe's migration guide says direct charges let connected accounts handle their own refunds and disputes. It also notes that, depending on configuration, negative-balance and fee responsibility can shift to the platform. Check against current docs. [secondary] — [Stripe config migration guide](https://edge-docs.stripe.com/connect/configuration-migration-guide)
- **Square** statements about platforms and direct charges: **not found** this session.
- **CSBS Money Transmission Modernization Act.** CSBS says 31 states have enacted it in full or in part (CSBS update April 2026; a surety-bond site gives the same count as of Sept 3, 2026). Virginia's full implementation took effect July 1, 2026. 2026 bills were introduced in AK, DE, LA, MI, and OK. Massachusetts (effective 2025) and Colorado (2025) include an agent-of-payee exemption. Third-party commentary places the exemption at MTMA §3.01(b) and lists three conditions: a written agreement, the payee holding the agent out as its agent, and payment to the agent treated as payment to the payee. [primary page + secondary] — [CSBS MTMA](https://www.csbs.org/csbs-money-transmission-modernization-act-mtma); [CSBS legislative update Apr 2026](https://www.csbs.org/mtma-legislative-update-4232026); [Stinson on MA](https://www.stinson.com/newsroom-publications-massachusetts-adopts-the-model-money-transmission-modernization-act-joining-25-other-states); [BHFS on Colorado](https://www.bhfs.com/insight/colorado-enacts-the-model-money-transmission-act/)
- **California DFPI/DBO opinion letters under Fin. Code §2010(l)** (added by AB 2209, effective Jan 1, 2015). There are agent-of-payee letters for an online games marketplace (May 8, 2017), an online platform where bands sell merchandise to fans (Oct 10, 2019), a payment processing service (Jan 27, 2020), and an online travel marketplace and independent-contractor marketplace (Jan 28, 2021). The common test: a written contract appointing the platform as the payee's agent, with the payor's payment fully satisfying the payor's obligation. The letters are limited to their facts. [primary letters located; read via excerpts] — [DFPI band-merch platform letter](https://dfpi.ca.gov/rules-enforcement/laws-and-regulations/opinion-letters-by-law-subject/agent-of-payee-exemption-online-platform-for-goods-or-services/); [DFPI games marketplace](https://dfpi.ca.gov/rules-enforcement/laws-and-regulations/opinion-letters-by-law-subject/exemption-for-agent-of-payee-online-games-marketplace/); [DFPI payment processing service PDF](https://dfpi.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/337/2020/02/Agent-of-Payee-Exemption-Payment-Processing-Service.pdf); [DBO 2021 letter](https://dbo.ca.gov/2021/02/25/agent-of-payee-exemption/)
- **Texas.** A 2017 law-firm comment letter to the DFPI says the Texas Department of Banking adopted a rule exempting processors that receive funds on behalf of a seller of goods or services (the 7 TAC §33.35 agent-of-payee rule). I did not open the current rule text. **New York**: secondary sources list NY among states that recognize agent-of-payee by policy or interpretation, but I found **no** DFS guidance specific to marketplaces. [secondary] — [Morrison & Foerster letter to DFPI](https://dfpi.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/337/2019/05/PRO-07-17-Morrison-and-Foerster-LLP.pdf); [Chicago Bar on agent-of-payee](https://cbaatthebar.chicagobar.org/2020/11/13/agent-of-the-payee-exemption/)
- **Law review.** Kevin V. Tu, "Regulating the New Cashless World," 65 *Alabama Law Review* 77 (2013). State money transmitter laws, written for Western Union-type businesses and defined broadly, create serious uncertainty for internet and mobile payment firms, and multistate compliance is "onerous and costly." [secondary summary] — [Regulatory Review summary](https://www.theregreview.org/2013/06/19/a-call-to-clarify-the-regulatory-scope-of-money-transmitter-laws/); [Ala. L. Rev. vol. 65](https://law.ua.edu/lawreview/volume-65/)
- Practitioner overviews: ABA *Business Law Today*, "Building a Payment Rail: Key Legal Considerations for Technology Platforms and Online Marketplaces" (June 2023, WSGR authors). Vermont's 2019 processor exclusion (written contract with payee plus BSA-regulated settlement). [secondary] — [ABA BLT 2023](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2023-june/building-a-payment-rail-key-legal-considerations); [Mondaq on Vermont](https://webiis08.mondaq.com/unitedstates/financial-services/810960/take-two-are-you-a-money-transmitter-in-vermont)

### Inferences
- OMP's strongest framing: the platform *never receives money for transmission*. Funds settle from Stripe to the organizer's own Standard account through direct charges, and the organizer is the merchant of record. This follows FIN-2014-R009's ISO reasoning and Stripe's MoR definitions, so the agent-of-payee exemption is a backstop, not the main argument.
- Two design choices would weaken this position: using destination or `on_behalf_of` charges (the platform takes negative-balance liability or becomes MoR), and holding funds in a platform balance before paying organizers. An `application_fee` taken by the platform is platform revenue, not transmitted funds. No source located here addresses that point directly, so mark it as needing counsel.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** FIN-2008-R006 content; any 2013 FinCEN ruling directly on marketplaces; a regulator ruling specifically on a platform using Standard accounts with direct charges; Square's statements; New York DFS's marketplace position; the current text of 7 TAC §33.35.
- **Not found:** a recent (2020–2026) law-review article focused on marketplaces and MTLs. Only Tu (2013) and practitioner pieces turned up.
- I did not obtain an authoritative state-by-state MTMA enactment list (only the count of 31).

## 6. Open-source adoption by small organizations and nonprofits

### Takeaway
Evidence here is thin. There is no current NTEN or TechSoup survey that measures open-source adoption among small nonprofits, and no study measuring hosted vs self-hosted preference in small organizations. The academic OSS-adoption literature centers on firms and the public sector. It consistently finds that organizational capacity, compatibility, and support matter more than license cost, and it documents abandonment cases such as the Munich LiMux reversal. Separate nonprofit data shows many small nonprofits have no IT staff, which supports offering a hosted default over expecting them to self-host.

### Cited Findings
- Hauge, Ayala & Conradi, "Adoption of open source software in software-intensive organizations – A systematic literature review," *Information and Software Technology* 52(11) (2010). 112 empirical papers from 1998–2008. Adoption involves far more than using OSS products, and the evidence base is fragmented. [abstract] — [UPC PDF](https://www.cs.upc.edu/~cayala/Papers/OSS-SLR-Hauge-Ayala2010.pdf)
- Rea Sánchez et al., systematic literature review of technical, organizational, and economic factors in FLOSS adoption, *IEEE Access* (Jan 2020). The lack of clear guidelines for IT managers puts adoption at risk. [abstract] — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/3bd50e443e8a46258a1d81fa07562c82); [idUS](https://hdl.handle.net/11441/126916)
- Linåker et al., "Open Source Software in the Public Sector: 25 years and still in its infancy" (arXiv 2308.04766, 2023, special-issue editorial). Regulatory, cultural, and capacity obstacles. [abstract] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04766)
- Fitzgerald, longitudinal case study of an Irish public hospital (2009). Some OSS applications were abandoned despite high assimilation. [abstract] — [FLOSShub](https://flosshub.org/node/1235)
- Munich LiMux. The council voted in Nov 2017 to return to Windows by 2020 after an Accenture review. Stated reasons were compatibility (SAP/Oracle/PDF), the cost of custom work, and staff complaints. The IT head disputed that there were "compelling technical reasons." In 2020 a new coalition said it prefers free software going forward. [secondary/news] — [Wikipedia: LiMux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LiMux); [Computing](https://computing.co.uk/news/3006338/city-of-munich-it-head-refutes-need-for-city-to-return-to-windows)
- Guoli Zhang, "Open source software adoption in non-profit organisations: socio-technical perspectives" (Edinburgh Napier PhD thesis, 2009). Nonprofits are often small and volunteer-run. This is the only nonprofit-specific OSS adoption study I found. [listing] — [Napier repository](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/output/216878/open-source-software-adoption-in-non-profit-organisations-socio-technical-perspectives)
- NTEN CMS satisfaction survey (2008). Open-source CMSs such as Plone rated well, but the sample was self-selected. Dated. [secondary] — [Plone list post](https://lists.plone.org/pipermail/plone-ngo/2008-May/001481.html)
- TechSoup global nonprofit tech surveys (2020 launch; 2021 report with about 12k respondents across 135 countries) cover digital readiness. I did not find OSS-specific results. [secondary] — [TechSoup blog](https://blog.techsoup.org/posts/a-critical-survey-for-nonprofits-responding-to-2020)
- Nonprofit IT capacity: a Moss Adams nonprofit survey (year not shown) found more than a third have no one responsible for IT and about a third assign it as a side duty. Chronicle of Philanthropy/Clarion (June 2025, n > 350) found a widening gap between nonprofits that can afford to modernize and those that can't. The often-quoted "60% have no full-time IT staff" (attributed to Tech Impact) is **unverified**. [secondary] — [Moss Adams PDF](https://WWW.MOSSADAMS.COM/getmedia/e3f2f858-667c-4ed0-891e-97e22988d673/NFP-Cybersecurity-and-Cloud-Technology.pdf); [Chronicle of Philanthropy 2025](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/5-key-findings-from-our-nonprofit-technology-survey/)

### Inferences
- The capacity data (many small nonprofits with no IT owner) and the Mastodon centralization finding (Q3) point the same way: OMP's main path should be the operator-hosted instance, with self-hosting for a few technically capable organizations or as an exit option.
- Munich and Fitzgerald suggest OSS is abandoned because of compatibility with surrounding systems and support burden, not license cost. For OMP, that means CSV/export compatibility with school and venue workflows (QuickBooks, Google Sheets, district finance) matters more than the fact that it is open source.

### Gaps
- **Not found:** any NTEN or TechSoup survey (2015–2026) that reports open-source adoption or abandonment rates among small nonprofits.
- **Not found:** any rigorous study of hosted vs self-hosted preference among small organizations. Only an 18-year-old vendor survey and an unpublished self-hosting study announcement turned up.
- **Not found:** studies on volunteer-run arts or music organizations' software adoption specifically.
