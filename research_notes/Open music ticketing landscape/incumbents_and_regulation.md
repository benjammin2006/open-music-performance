## 1. Live Nation Entertainment / Ticketmaster: market share, vertical integration, exclusive contracts, the flywheel

### Takeaway
Ticketmaster has ~75% of all primary ticketing and ~86% of primary *concert* ticketing at "major concert venues" (arenas/amphitheaters of 8,000+ capacity with 10+ concerts/yr), a share stable since 2017, locked in by 3-5 year mostly-exclusive contracts in which Ticketmaster pays venues upfront money plus a majority share of the fan-paid fees; Live Nation's promotion and venue businesses (incl. 125 clubs and 112 theaters) supply the content leverage that makes venues fear switching. A court record exists of venues being told concerts might move if they chose a different ticketer.

### Cited Findings

**Scale (Live Nation FY2025 10-K, filed Feb 2026)**
- FY2025 segment revenue: Concerts $20.9B (83%), Ticketing $3.1B (12%, net of fees paid to clients and excluding face value), Sponsorship & Advertising $1.3B (5%) — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Total 2025 revenue $25.2B (+9%), AOI $2.4B (+10%); 159M fans attended; concert GTV $26B — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm); [MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/live-nation-annual-revenues-top-25b-in-2025-up-2b-yoy-with-adjusted-operating-profit-of-2-4b)
- Ticketing segment: ~$3.1B revenue, ~$1.1B AOI in 2025 (i.e., ~35% AOI margin vs. concerts' thin margin) — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Ticketmaster distributed 646M tickets in 2025: ~346M fee-bearing and ~300M "sold without a fee paid for services"; fee-bearing GTV $37.1B (+6%); 10,500 clients worldwide — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Concerts: 55,000 events, 11,000+ artists, 131 festivals in 2025; ~360 artists managed (artist management arm) — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Sponsorship: ~1,500 sponsors, ~830-person sales force — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- **Venue portfolio as of 2025-12-31: 460 venues** — 11 stadiums, 78 amphitheaters, 41 arenas, **112 theaters, 125 clubs**, 67 outdoor spaces, 26 other (40 owned, 412 operated/leased, 8 equity interest) — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Q2 2026: revenue $7.7B (+9%); Ticketing revenue $852.2M (+15%), Ticketing AOI $331.0M (+14%); fee-bearing tickets 90.1M (vs 83.3M); fee-bearing GTV $10.4B (vs $9.1B); concerts attendance ~49M (+10%) — [LYV Q2 2026 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001335258/000133525826000035/lyv-20260630.htm); [LYV Q2 2026 release](https://news.livenationentertainment.com/news/live-nation-entertainment-reports-second-quarter-2026-results); [Zacks](https://www.zacks.com/stock/news/2966012/live-nations-q2-earnings-revenues-beat-estimates-rise-yy)
- 6-month 2026 operating income $151.4M vs $601.4M prior year, with corporate expenses $666.1M vs $234.0M — consistent with a large legal accrual — [LYV Q2 2026 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001335258/000133525826000035/lyv-20260630.htm)
- Live Nation recognized a ~$450M legal reserve/accrual in H1 2026 as its best estimate of loss from the settling states and the jury award — [Hypebot, May 2026](https://www.hypebot.com/live-nation-legal-battles-where-three-major-cases-stand-now.md); [settlementinsight](https://settlementinsight.com/live-nation-lawsuit)

**Market share (court record, Feb 18, 2026 summary-judgment opinion, SDNY 24-cv-3973)**
- "Major concert venue" (MCV) = arena or large amphitheater, capacity 8,000+, that hosted 10+ concerts in at least one year 2017-2024 — [SDNY SJ Opinion, 2026-02-18](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Ticketmaster share at MCVs: 75% of primary ticketing, 86% of primary *concert* ticketing; Live Nation did not contest these figures; government calculates share "well over 80%" by tickets sold, "stable since 2017" — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- 65% of MCVs had 100%-exclusive Ticketmaster contracts in 2024; 71% if threshold is ≥95% (to allow carve-outs); "upward of 70%" of Ticketmaster contracts have an exclusivity provision — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Only 3% of MCVs self-ticket; testimony that "ticketing systems are generally too expensive and too difficult for venues to build themselves" — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Live Nation has ~80% share of "major concert amphitheaters" (8,000+ cap) — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Live Nation argued its share is ~44% under a broader market (incl. stadiums, smaller amphitheaters, large theaters) — [trial coverage via search summary; Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/live-nation-verdict-everything-we-know-1235548524/) (figure not independently verified)
- Internal Ticketmaster documents: exclusive ticketing at 84% of NBA/NHL arenas; goal to "maintain majority market share of NBA and NHL" — [Big Tech on Trial, Day 19/20, 2026-04-03](https://www.bigtechontrial.com/p/day-19-ticketmaster-exec-acknowledges)
- Club/small-venue market: the court record shows testimony that ticketing a "black box club" is "a very different operation lift," that clubs/large theaters are general-admission with different needs, and that "the set of companies that provide ticketing services is different between smaller venues and arenas" — the antitrust case and its market shares cover only 8,000+ cap venues; **no court/regulatory market-share figure exists for clubs/small venues** — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)

**How exclusive venue ticketing contracts work**
- 10-K: Ticketmaster "generally gets paid a fixed fee per ticket sold or a percentage of the total ticket service charges"; it pays clients "generally in the form of upfront payments, a portion of service fee revenue and the portion of other services at low or no cost"; agreements typically run 3-5 years — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Court: "Ticketers typically pay the venue in exchange for the (usually exclusive) right to sell tickets on their behalf over multiple years... On top of that, the ticketer returns a large portion of the fees from the fans to the venue." Live Nation's own expert said exclusivity justifies "large up-front payments" — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Venue testimony that switching to exclusive ticketing was "to allow [the] venues to participate and receive a percentage of the service charges" (i.e., fee-share is the venue's motive) — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Ticketmaster says the venue normally gets ~two-thirds of the service charge and often a facility fee too — [Ticketmaster blog "The Truth About Ticket Prices"](https://blog.ticketmaster.com/the-truth-about-ticket-prices/) (interested party)
- Sales-staff incentives (to at least 2024): bonuses tied to renewals; contracts signed >6 months before expiration paid higher bonuses; a "term length multiplier" rewarded longer terms. Internal Slack: "continue to aggressively pursue early renewal opportunities to avoid RFP process." Ticketmaster VP said extensions are usually venue-requested to fund upfront payments for capital improvements — [Big Tech on Trial, 2026-04-03](https://www.bigtechontrial.com/p/day-19-ticketmaster-exec-acknowledges)
- Retaliation evidence: venue deponent: "We were told that if we chose a different ticketing partner that the possibility of concerts moving to a different venue was a distinct possibility... Q: Did you consider this statement a threat? A: Yes." SeatGeek signed "make good" clauses paying venues if Live Nation retaliates — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf); SeatGeek told the court it offered "retaliation insurance" to eight venues — [Pollstar, 2026-09-15](https://news.pollstar.com/2026/09/15/the-biz-objections-roll-in-as-judge-reviews-ln-settlement-umg-wraps-buyback/)
- Live Nation kept a money-losing amphitheater (Pavilion at Montage Mountain, Scranton) because "a decision was made to not give up a venue which competitors could snap up" — [Big Tech on Trial](https://www.bigtechontrial.com/p/day-19-ticketmaster-exec-acknowledges)
- **Oak View Group "kickback" channel**: Ticketmaster paid OVG a $20M upfront signing bonus plus up to $7.5M/yr over 10 years (another outlet says $7M) to steer OVG-managed venues to Ticketmaster; OVG was to push 60/40 venue/Ticketmaster fee splits at switching venues and ≥2%/yr increases in Ticketmaster's fee share at renewals; OVG did not disclose this to venue clients (DOJ non-prosecution agreement found breach of fiduciary duty) — [TheTicketingBusiness, 2025-07](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2025/07/doj-alleges-ticketing-provider-paid-millions-to-ovg-for-pushing-its-services/); [Big Tech on Trial Day 14](https://www.bigtechontrial.com/p/day-14-company-paid-70-million-to); [MBW](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/nightmare-for-live-nation-questions-asked-as-doj-discovers-ticketing-kickbacks-in-ovg-boss-bid-rigging-probe/). Connecticut audit (Aug 2026) found OVG bypassed bidding rules for a Ticketmaster deal at Hartford's XL Center — [TicketNews, 2026-08](https://www.ticketnews.com/2026/08/connecticut-audit-confirms-ovg-bypassed-bidding-rules-for-ticketmaster-deal-at-hartfords-xl-center/)
- Promoter-venue rebates: venues "often pay higher rebates to LN when LN or its affiliates deliver more shows" (government theory; court called it "just a description of an incentive contract") — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- Promotion economics: promoters pay artists lump-sum guarantees, take the risk, then split profits; venues pay promoters for booking (per ticket, per show, or cost/revenue splits) — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)
- [HISTORICAL] 2010 merger consent decree barred retaliation against venues using rival ticketers; amended/extended Dec 2019 after DOJ found violations — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Live_Nation_Entertainment)

### Inferences
- The flywheel: Live Nation controls tours (promotion) and venues → venues fear losing LN shows if they leave Ticketmaster → Ticketmaster wins long exclusive deals by paying venues upfront cash + ~2/3 of fan fees → fee revenue (35% AOI margin) subsidizes artist guarantees and venue acquisition → more content control. The venue's economic incentive is aligned with *higher* fan fees, which is the central structural problem a fee-transparent alternative must confront: venues may prefer the incumbent precisely because it pays them more out of fan fees.
- The "upfront payment" (advance) is a financing product. Small venues with thin capital are especially susceptible: the advance funds renovations/COVID survival, and repayment is structured through the multi-year exclusive. An open-source alternative cannot easily match capital advances; it would need to compete on lower total fees, data ownership, and no exclusivity.
- Live Nation operates 125 clubs and 112 theaters (House of Blues/Fillmore-type rooms), so it competes directly with independent clubs for touring artists in the sub-3,000 tier; but the antitrust case and every remedy are framed around 8,000+ cap venues. Small-venue lock-in is legally unaddressed.

### Gaps
- No credible source found giving Ticketmaster's market share in clubs/theaters (<3,000 cap) or for Ticketmaster's club-oriented brand TicketWeb.
- Typical dollar size of signing advances for small/mid venues not found; 10-K does not disclose amounts.
- Full 10-K segment AOI breakdown and ticketing-contract advances balance were not extracted (10-K is 250K characters; only first 100K read).

## 2. United States v. Live Nation Entertainment (SDNY 1:24-cv-03973-AS): status as of 2026-10-14

### Takeaway
DOJ settled mid-trial (announced 2026-03-09) without a Ticketmaster divestiture; 33 states + DC continued and **won a unanimous jury verdict on 2026-04-15** (Ticketmaster monopolized primary ticketing at major concert venues; Live Nation monopolized large amphitheaters; unlawful tying; $1.72/ticket overcharge). As of 2026-10-14: **the DOJ settlement's Tunney Act approval is PENDING** (ruling expected mid-Sept to mid/late-Oct 2026, none found), **Live Nation's post-trial Rule 50(b)/new-trial motions are PENDING**, and **the remedies/penalties bench trial (states seek Ticketmaster divestiture) is expected no earlier than Feb 2027**. No divestiture has been ordered. Appeals to the Second Circuit are expected after judgment; final resolution unlikely before 2028.

## 3. Fee structures, who keeps what, and the regulatory regime (FTC rule, TICKET Act, state laws, BOTS Act)

### Takeaway
Fees on primary tickets averaged ~27% of face value (GAO 2018 sample; ~30% for concerts) and ~31% on resale; Ticketmaster says venues set fees and keep ~2/3 of the service charge. Since 2025-05-12 the FTC Fees Rule requires all-in price display for live-event tickets but does not cap fees; the federal TICKET Act passed the House 409-15 (Apr 2025) but has not passed the Senate as of the latest sources. States are the active front: MD and MN (2024 laws: all-in pricing + speculative-ticket bans), VT and DC resale caps, NY extended its existing law to 2027-06-30 without new caps.

### Cited Findings

**Fee levels**
- GAO-18-347 (Apr/May 2018, nongeneralizable sample): primary fees averaged 27% of ticket price, secondary 31%; white-label resale sites sometimes >40%; concerts/theater ~30% vs sports ~20% on primary platforms — [GAO-18-347](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-347) [HISTORICAL]

- Eventbrite: 3.7% + $1.79/ticket (Essentials), 6.2% + $1.79 (Professional), free for free events, plus ~2.9% payment processing — [TicketFairy comparison 2026](https://www.ticketfairy.com/event-ticketing/eventbrite-vs-dice); [TickPick organizer guide](https://www.tickpick.com/blog/organizer/ticketing-fees-explained) [VENDOR CLAIM; published rates vary by source]

## 6. Where ticket revenue goes (worked examples) and what incumbents ignore

### Takeaway
On a $100 arena ticket, a credible advocacy estimate leaves the artist ~$41 gross / ~$8 net after costs, with ~$28 in fees split among venue, promoter and ticketer and none to the artist; no credible worked example for a $30 club ticket was found. Incumbents' antitrust markets and remedies target only 8,000+ cap venues; small/no-fee events are where they are thinnest.

### Cited Findings
- NITO $100 example: ~$28 fees (to venue/promoter/ticketer, not artist); ~$30 staging/production/staff; remaining $48 split ~85/15 → artist ~$40.80, promoter/venue ~$7.20; artist costs eat 75-85% → ~$8.16 artist profit (estimates) — [Hypebot, Dec 2024](https://www.hypebot.com/hypebot/2024/12/what-artists-earn-from-ticket-sales-a-revealing-revenue-breakdown.html). Conflict: a WSJ graphic claimed artists get up to 98% of ticket revenue, disputed by NITO — same source.
- Ticketmaster's view: face value goes mostly to performers, then show costs, promoters last; venue sets and keeps ~2/3 of service fee — [Ticketmaster blog](https://blog.ticketmaster.com/the-truth-about-ticket-prices/)
- Jury: $1.72/ticket overcharge in fees at major concert venues attributable to monopoly — [NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-coalition-states-win-trial-against-live-nation-and)
- 10-K: ~300M of 646M Ticketmaster tickets (2025) were distributed without a service fee (e.g., client/box-office/no-fee distribution) — [LYV FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1335258/000133525826000009/lyv-20251231.htm)
- Small venue ticketing: Ticketmaster "coverage for small independent rooms remains limited"; Eventbrite/Ticket Tailor/DICE/Etix dominate sub-500 rooms; TicketWeb is Ticketmaster-owned — [TickPick guide](https://www.tickpick.com/blog/organizer/best-ticketing-platform-small-venues) [VENDOR CLAIM]
- Eventbrite charges nothing for free events — [TicketFairy](https://www.ticketfairy.com/event-ticketing/eventbrite-vs-dice)
- Court testimony: ticketing clubs is "a very different operation lift"; arena ticketing needs (reserved seats, season tickets, bot defense) differ from GA clubs — [SDNY SJ Opinion](https://www.nysd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/24cv3973%20Opinion%20&%20Order.pdf)

### Inferences
- Incumbent economics depend on per-ticket fees as % of price; free events, school/community shows, sub-300 rooms, and pay-at-door/non-ticketed shows generate little fee revenue and are lightly served (Ticketmaster) or served by self-serve SaaS (Eventbrite etc.). This is the plausible beachhead, but it is also where the incumbent "lock-in" is weakest, so the competition is with Eventbrite/DICE/Ticket Tailor more than Ticketmaster.
- For a $30 club ticket, applying the GAO ~27-30% average would imply ~$8-9 in fees; this is an extrapolation, not a sourced example.

### Gaps
- No credible sourced worked example for a $30 club ticket.
- No data on share of US live music that is free/non-ticketed or under 300 capacity.
